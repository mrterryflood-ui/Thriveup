The Core Design Principle

"This is DATA, not characterization." Every report begins with a disclaimer that these are statistical characteristics of a geographic area, not judgments of its residents. Every neighborhood has strengths and challenges. The data is meant to inform compassionate action.

Most data tools build dashboards for policymakers. ThriveUp is built so that a community health worker at a grocery store, a pastor after Sunday service, or a parent at a PTA meeting can pull up a compassionate neighborhood profile on their phone and walk through specific solutions — in under 5 seconds, without training, in their language.

The Neighborhood Intelligence Engine

The flagship tool transforms any location input — a ZIP code, a street name, a neighborhood name, even "East Austin" — into a compassionate, actionable data story. It:

Pulls live data from the U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey across 15 social vulnerability indicators

Computes a Social Vulnerability Index score using CDC/ATSDR methodology across 4 themes (Socioeconomic, Household Composition, Minority Status, Housing & Transportation)

Identifies what's going well — community strengths to celebrate, not just gaps to fix

Flags what needs attention — with specific, actionable solutions (not just data, but what to do about it)

Matches community needs against up to 20 live federal grant opportunities from SAM.gov

Generates a downloadable 10-page PDF action plan and a 15–20 slide scripted PowerPoint presentation ready for City Council meetings, church gatherings, or PTA meetings

"What If" Scenario Sandbox — adjust sliders for poverty, unemployment, graduation rates, insurance, transportation, and broadband, and see in real time how the vulnerability score changes

The Federal Data Foundation

ThriveUp integrates 8 federal data sources in real time, each traceable through ChainWeb — the citation-traceable evidence engine where every claim chains back to a primary federal URL:

Census Bureau ACS (15 social vulnerability variables) · CDC PLACES (local health outcomes) · CDC/ATSDR SVI (4-theme vulnerability index) · FBI Crime Data API (community safety) · USDA Food Access Atlas (food desert identification) · HUD (housing affordability) · SAMHSA (behavioral health locator) · BLS (labor market and wages)

955 live federal data connections across 67+ agencies. Adding new geographies requires zero code changes — the Census Bureau API serves all 50 states. Cost per additional geography approaches zero.

What This Means for Data Storytelling Training

The platform's storytelling methodology follows three rules that should anchor any data storytelling training:

1. Lead with the human, anchor with the data. Maria's story opens every benefits conversation. The $8,400 figure follows. Not the other way around. Data without a face is abstract; a face without data is anecdote. You need both, in that order.

2. Strengths before challenges. Every Neighborhood Intelligence report surfaces what's going well before it flags what needs attention. This is not politeness — it is strategy. A community that hears only its deficits becomes defensive. A community that hears its strengths first opens up.

3. Every number has a source chain. ChainWeb exists because "trust me" fails in funder rooms, city council chambers, and community meetings. If you can't show the URL, don't say the number. This discipline also catches your own errors before they leave the room.
