The Core Problem

Williamson County is Texas's fastest-growing large county — population up 78% from 2010 to 2023. The infrastructure that grew with it was roads, warehouses, and semiconductor fabs (Samsung Taylor, Applied Materials Hutto). What did not grow: licensed infant care, subsidy-accepting childcare providers, or extended-hour care for shift workers.

The result is a childcare desert that nobody mapped because the median household income looks fine on a county-wide chart. The crisis is real; it's just hidden inside averages.

What the 8% figure actually means: The 8% reflects the share of income-eligible children (families at or below 85% State Median Income) actively enrolled in childcare with a Texas Rising Star-certified provider under a TWC subsidy. This is not a floor — it is a ceiling under current provider supply. The gap between 8% (WilCo today) and 29% (Bexar County) represents what intentional system-building can accomplish over time.

Where the System Is Broken

Provider Supply Has Not Kept Pace

WilCo's population nearly doubled, but licensed childcare capacity — particularly at 2★+ TRS level — has not scaled. New employers (Samsung, Applied Materials) entering the Round Rock, Cedar Park, and Georgetown corridors face a workforce constrained by childcare access. Infant slots (newborn–18m) are the primary bottleneck.

Shift-Worker Fit Is Broken

Samsung Taylor and Applied Materials Hutto run 12-hour rotating DuPont schedules (2-2-3). Almost no licensed center operates outside 6:30am–6:30pm. Family childcare is the only option — and family childcare with infant care is rare. 26,000+ semiconductor jobs with no childcare solution is a workforce crisis.

Subsidy Access Is Patchy

Workforce Solutions Rural Capital Area runs the local CCS contract. Providers who accept subsidy concentrate in older Round Rock and Georgetown corridors. Subsidy-accepting capacity in Liberty Hill/Leander north of US-183A is thin. WilCo families in high-growth suburban ZIP codes face longer waits as the slot pool does not proportionally account for county-level population shifts.

Language and Special-Needs Gaps

Williamson County's Hispanic population share keeps growing; bilingual provider count has not. Texas Rising Star 4-star providers with staffing ratios for ECI-eligible infants concentrate in Cedar Park and central Round Rock — rural WilCo is underserved.

The Data Story — What Tells It

The most powerful number in this case is the gap between 8% and 29%. That 21-point gap is not a failure of families — it is a failure of infrastructure. Bexar County didn't get to 29% because San Antonio families tried harder. They got there because United Way, anchor institutions, and county government built a system over 15 years.

North Williamson County has something Bexar didn't in 2010: 26,000 semiconductor jobs creating immediate, employer-driven pressure to solve this. Samsung and Applied Materials need their workers to show up. Workers can't show up if their infant has nowhere safe to go at 6:30am on a rotating shift. That is not a social services story. That is an economic development story — and it opens entirely different funding doors.

The replication story: what we build in WilCo can replicate to every fast-growth suburban county in Texas (Hays, Denton, Collin, Fort Bend) that has the same semiconductor/logistics/healthcare employer base and the same invisible childcare desert.
