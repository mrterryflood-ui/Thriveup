### Q1 — Community Need & Context (25 pts)

**What issue(s) affecting immigrant/refugee communities are you addressing and why is it important in Austin right now?**

About one in three Austin residents speaks a language other than English at home. A meaningful share of those households have limited English proficiency. The city has grown faster than the supply of services that meet people in the language and dialect that feels like home — and the gap falls hardest on the Eastern Crescent, where immigrant household density is high and displacement pressure is highest.

We are not naming an abstract need. We are naming what immigrant families in our coalition tell us happens to them in a normal week:

- A parent gets a school discipline notice and cannot tell whether it requires a response, a meeting, or a lawyer.
- A tenant gets an eviction notice with a 3-day deadline and does not know that responding the wrong way forfeits the case.
- A worker gets a wage-theft document and cannot identify Workers Defense Project as the right place to call.
- A grandmother who speaks Vietnamese watches her grandchild translate a medical consent form she does not understand — and signs anyway.
- A Deaf resident in a hospital intake room is handed a tablet with no interpreter — and there is no Black ASL signer on the platform she was offered, only generic ASL that is not how she signs at home.
- A DeafBlind resident at a benefits office has no Tactile Sign option in the room at all.

Existing tools fail in three specific ways our partners describe again and again. They flatten dialect, so the reply does not sound like home. They translate the words but not the stakes — Google Translate will render an eviction notice in Spanish, but it will not tell you that BASTA Austin is the number to call. And they have no relationship to Austin: no feedback loop, no accountability, no obligation to the people who use them.

Austin already has remarkable immigrant-serving organizations — Catholic Charities of Central Texas, Refugee Services of Texas, Multicultural Refugee Coalition, Casa Marianella, El Buen Samaritano, RAICES, Workers Defense Project, Foundation Communities, AVANCE, Literacy Austin, and Sixth Square. The problem is not absence of services. The problem is **agency** — the day-to-day capacity for a resident to see what is available, communicate in the language and dialect that feels like home, and move between services without re-explaining their situation each time.

We know this from sustained work, not assumption. TCAF has been in conversation with each of the eleven coalition partners listed in Q4 over the past year. The five patterns above are not invented; they are composites drawn from what frontline staff at those organizations describe as their most common Monday-morning calls. We have also listened directly through Voices of Austin, our existing multilingual storytelling space, and through the resource requests our LifeBridge navigation platform has logged from Eastern Crescent ZIPs.

The reason this matters now is timing. Austin's immigrant communities are facing tighter housing pressure, more frequent enforcement encounters, and a federal landscape that makes trustworthy local navigation more urgent each month. A small, plainly-built, community-accountable tool — embedded in the CBOs people already trust — can close the agency gap before more families lose ground.

### Q2 — The Work & Use of Funds (25 pts)

**What work are you seeking funding for and how will funds be used?**

We are seeking $25,000 to fund the **community-facing work** of Talk Your Talk in Austin's Eastern Crescent over 12–15 months. The technology platforms (Talk Your Talk multilingual interpreter, LifeBridge resource navigator, Voices of Austin storytelling, and our mental health navigator) are already built, deployed, and operating at zero cost to this grant. Every dollar of the $25,000 goes into community labor and materials that put the platform in residents' hands.

**Budget — $25,000 total**

| Line | Amount | What it pays for |
|---|---:|---|
| Two part-time Community Navigators | $10,400 | A Spanish/English navigator and a Haitian Creole/English (or Vietnamese/English, based on coalition need). About 8 hours/week each × 12 months × $25/hr. Rotating placements at 4 partner CBOs. They run weekly walk-in clinics, train residents on the document explainer and Know Your Rights features in person, and handle warm handoffs to legal aid, clinics, schools, and resettlement agencies. **This is how the platform reaches people.** |
| Printed Know Your Rights cards | $4,500 | 5 scenarios (Medical/ER, School, Housing, Workplace, Police/ICE) × 6 priority languages × 2,000 sets = **60,000 physical cards**. Sourced from ACLU, NILC, and Immigrant Defense Project. Distributed at libraries, schools, clinics, food pantries, faith communities, and day-labor sites in Eastern Crescent ZIPs (78702, 78721, 78723, 78724, 78744, 78753, 78758). Each card carries a QR code linking to the live, updatable digital version. |
| 12 in-language community workshops | $4,800 | Hosted at partner CBOs, monthly. $400 per workshop covers a host stipend to the partner org, **childcare, food, and materials**. Participants leave with the app installed, an account created, and their first three documents already explained. Stipending the partner org respects their labor instead of treating it as free. |
| Translation review honoraria | $2,400 | $400 each for community translators in 6 priority languages who review AI output for cultural and dialect accuracy. **The people whose linguistic expertise the platform depends on get paid.** |
| Accessibility audit with paid disabled testers | $1,400 | Third-party WCAG 2.2 AA audit including paid Deaf, Hard-of-Hearing, and screen-reader testers. We do not ask for free quality assurance from the people the audit is meant to serve. |
| Evaluation and renewal-funder evidence packet | $1,500 | Configures our evaluation tools to track this pilot, produces simple quarterly reports for the Equity Office, and produces a year-end evidence packet sized to support follow-on funding applications so this work can continue past the grant period. |
| **Total** | **$25,000** | |

**What the platform actually does (already built, in residents' hands the day funds land).**

Talk Your Talk covers **89 spoken languages and 18 sign languages — 107 total**. The sign-language list intentionally includes **Black ASL as a separate entry** (most platforms erase it by folding it into ASL), **International Sign** for refugee and cross-border use, and **Tactile Sign** for DeafBlind residents — three coverage decisions that matter in Austin and that mainstream tools miss. Spoken-language coverage includes dialect variants residents actually speak at home: 10 English variants (including AAVE, Gullah Geechee, Appalachian, and Spanglish), 8 Spanish variants (Mexican, Dominican, Puerto Rican, Cuban, Colombian, Argentine, Castilian, Central American), 6 Arabic variants, and dialect-aware coverage for French, Portuguese, and Mandarin.

Six everyday surfaces, each accessible from the resident's phone or installed on their home screen as an offline-capable app:

- **Real-time interpretation** with **crisis detection on every utterance** — five severity levels, in-language keyword recall plus AI analysis. When severity rises to *severe* or *critical*, the app surfaces an alert with one-tap dialer access to **911** and **988**. We are explicit with funders: the platform does not auto-dispatch to 988 (their public API does not allow that) and does not yet route to a live human interpreter (next-cycle line item) — what ships is detection, structured outcome data, and immediate dialer access.
- **Snap & Learn** — point your camera at a document, sign, or label and get a bilingual flashcard in your home dialect.
- **Spaced-repetition review** — Anki-style SM-2 algorithm so newly learned vocabulary actually sticks.
- **Match Game** — tap-the-photo learning through play (works for early childhood and adult learners alike).
- **Live Learning Sessions** — Kahoot-style classroom with a 6-letter join code, designed for partner CBOs to run multilingual workshops.
- **Belonging Path** — a 12-unit guided journey for residents new to Austin's systems.
- **Family Circles + Mentor Match** — household-level pairing and 1:1 mentor matching with longer-settled community members.

**The behavioral-health safety floor underneath all of this.** Talk Your Talk does not stand alone. It sits on top of our **Whole-Person Health Ecosystem** (mentalwellnesssupport.net), the no-login behavioral-health hub that catches residents when removing the language barrier is not enough by itself. The hub delivers validated clinical screenings (C-SSRS for suicidality, PHQ-9 for depression, GAD-7 for anxiety, PCL-5 for PTSD), individualized safety plans with auto-escalation, a "Reach a Vet" crisis pathway, MAP-GAP biopsychosocial assessment, and 20,670+ curated resources across 60 condition guides and 19 population-specific hubs — all available offline through an installable PWA, with no account required. Every crisis-detection event Talk Your Talk surfaces is routed into this hub for follow-up so that detection turns into care, not just a dialer screen. This matters for AEI because immigrant and refugee residents disproportionately face untreated trauma, post-migration grief, family separation stress, and provider mistrust; a Spanish-only or Arabic-only resident who reaches a screener in their own language and finds a no-login, free, validated tool on the other side is dramatically more likely to actually use it.

**Why this fits AEI priorities.** The work expands access to essential services in residents' own languages and dialects, reduces barriers (cost, English-only forms, child interpreters, intimidation), strengthens belonging through Voices of Austin and the workshops, and contributes to long-term stability by connecting residents to workforce, legal, housing, and health navigation. We are not duplicating City services — we are building the language and trust layer that helps residents reach the services Austin already offers.

**Stewardship.** The $25,000 is one-time. By spending it on community labor, printed materials, and partner stipends — not on overhead, salaries above the navigator line, or technology we already maintain — we get a high return per dollar and leave permanent assets behind: 60,000 cards in residents' hands, 12 partner-hosted workshops on the calendar, paid relationships with community translators and disabled testers, and an evidence packet that helps us pursue continuation funding without asking the City to repeat this investment.

### Q3 — Community Leadership (25 pts)

**How are immigrants/refugees involved in leading, shaping, or evaluating this work?**

Talk Your Talk is being shaped, in real time, by the immigrant and refugee residents and the immigrant- and refugee-led organizations it serves. We want to be plain about what that looks like in practice and what we are still building.

**What community leadership looks like today.**

The pilot's design is the product of a year of conversations with eleven Austin immigrant-serving organizations: Multicultural Refugee Coalition, Foundation Communities, Casa Marianella, AVANCE-Austin, Catholic Charities of Central Texas, Literacy Austin, Refugee Services of Texas, Workers Defense Project, RAICES, Sixth Square, and El Buen Samaritano. Several of these organizations are immigrant- and refugee-led at the staff and board level. The five "Monday-morning patterns" we name in Q1 are not assumptions — they were named to us by frontline staff at these CBOs, most of whom share lived immigrant or refugee experience with the communities they serve. Their feedback has changed the project: the priority language list, the six phrase-board scenarios, the decision to pay translators rather than rely on volunteers, and the explicit choice not to frame this as ESL remediation are all responses to what coalition partners told us.

The two Community Navigators funded by this grant will be hired from the served communities themselves — recruited through partner CBOs, with coalition partners on the hiring panel. The navigators will be paid, not volunteer; they will be embedded inside partner organizations rather than at TCAF; and they will set the workshop topics each month based on what residents are actually walking in asking about.

**A community advisory circle, formalized in Month 1.**

In the first month of the grant, we will formally seat a **Community Advisory Circle** of 6–8 immigrant and refugee leaders — including coalition partner staff and unaffiliated community members nominated by partner CBOs. Members are paid an honorarium for their time. The Circle has real authority: it approves the priority language list each quarter, approves the workshop topic calendar, reviews translation accuracy disputes, and signs off on the year-end evidence packet before it is shared with the Equity Office or any future funder.

**Power-sharing on the platform itself.**

The Voices of Austin storytelling space is already a place where immigrant and refugee residents shape the public narrative about their own communities, in their own words and dialects. Stories are recorded and preserved with explicit consent, in the resident's home language, with the resident — not TCAF — controlling whether and how their story is shared. The Mentor Match feature pairs longer-settled immigrants with newly arrived families from the same country of origin, with the mentor relationship structured around the mentee's questions, not a curriculum we wrote.

**What we are honest about.**

TCAF's senior leadership is Black-led and veteran-founded; it is not led by immigrants or refugees. We recognize that this is a real distinction. Our role in this pilot is to remove infrastructure cost from immigrant- and refugee-led partners and put resources directly into community-facing labor, materials, and decision-making seats — not to speak for the community. The Community Advisory Circle, the partner CBO coalition, and the embedded navigator model are how we structure that accountability into the work, not just into the application.

### Q4 — Relationships & Impact (25 pts)

**How are you accountable to the community you serve? How do you understand and evaluate your impact?**

**Relationships first.**

TCAF has worked alongside Austin's immigrant-serving ecosystem long enough to know that trust is built one organization, one staff person, and one family at a time — and that it can be broken in a single week of bad behavior. Our accountability practice has three layers:

1. **To the partner CBO coalition.** We meet monthly with our coalition partners (Multicultural Refugee Coalition, Foundation Communities, Casa Marianella, AVANCE-Austin, Catholic Charities of Central Texas, Literacy Austin, Refugee Services of Texas, Workers Defense Project, RAICES, Sixth Square, and El Buen Samaritano). Each partner has a standing invitation to flag anything about the platform — accuracy, tone, distribution, navigator behavior — that does not feel right, and we commit to answering within 5 business days and changing course where the partner's reading is correct. We are transparent about a real example of this in practice: El Buen Samaritano informed us they are also applying under this same FY26 cycle, and we disclose that openly here rather than hide it.

2. **To the Community Advisory Circle (described in Q3).** The Circle has approval authority over the priority language list, workshop calendar, translation dispute resolution, and the year-end evidence packet. Their decisions are recorded; if we override them, we have to explain in writing, in plain language, to the Circle and to our coalition partners.

3. **To the residents themselves.** Every workshop ends with a 3-question listening prompt in the resident's home language: *Did this help? What would have made it better? What should we do next?* Responses are anonymized, summarized, and posted to a public `/our-impact` page so residents see their feedback shaping what comes next.

**How we understand impact.**

We try to be honest about what we count and what we do not. We do not report "estimated lives changed" or "potential impact." We count what actually happens.

The simple counts we will report quarterly to the Equity Office:

- Residents onboarded to the platform (with self-reported language and ZIP)
- Documents explained through the platform (medical, school, housing, government)
- Documents flagged for legal help where the resident clicked through to a legal-aid partner
- Family Circles formed (households linking 2 or more members)
- Mentor pairings activated and still active at 6 and 12 months
- Workshops held, attendance, childcare provided
- Know Your Rights cards distributed (inventory tracked)
- **Languages and dialects of users actually served** — counted from onboarding across the 89 spoken and 18 sign languages, not assumed
- **Snap & Learn flashcards generated** and **spaced-repetition review sessions completed** (vocabulary-acquisition outcome data)
- **Live Learning Sessions hosted** by partner CBOs (with 6-letter join code participation counts)
- **Crisis-detection events** by severity level and language (queryable from the platform's structured outcome data — supports honest safety reporting to the Equity Office)
- Accessibility audit completed and findings addressed

Alongside the counts, we report stories. With consent, we share short, anonymized accounts of residents who used the platform at a moment that mattered — a school meeting, a clinic visit, an eviction notice — and what happened. Stories are reviewed by the Community Advisory Circle before publication. We never share details that could identify a vulnerable resident or expose anyone to risk; the safety-first framing the Equity Office names in the playbook is non-negotiable for us.

**What we do with what we learn.**

If something is not working, we say so — in the next quarterly report and to our coalition partners — and we change it. The year-end evidence packet is built from this same honest record, which is why it can also be used to pursue continuation funding without overstating what the pilot did. We would rather report a smaller honest number than a larger one we cannot defend.
