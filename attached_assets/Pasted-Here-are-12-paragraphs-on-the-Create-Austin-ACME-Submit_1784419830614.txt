Here are 12 paragraphs on the Create Austin ACME Submittable portal walkthrough at createaustin.org:

1. Who Runs It and Why It Exists
Create Austin is a program of the Long Center's Community Programs Department, operating under a contract with the City of Austin to administer the Austin Arts, Culture, Music and Entertainment (ACME) funding programs. The Long Center has managed this grant cycle for several years on the City's behalf, serving as the administrative backbone between the City of Austin's cultural funding priorities and the artists, musicians, and cultural organizations on the ground. The website createaustin.org serves as the public resource hub — the place applicants go to understand what programs exist, what each requires, and how to navigate the application system before they ever log into Submittable.

2. The Scale of Funding Available
For FY27, ACME has opened more than $23 million in grant funding through this cycle alone — part of a historically unprecedented $51 million in total grants the City of Austin is distributing across the full fiscal year. Grants range from $5,000 to $250,000 depending on the program and the applicant's budget size. This is not small-dollar discretionary money; it is a significant public investment in Austin's creative economy, distributed through a competitive, structured review process. All of it flows from Hotel Occupancy Tax (HOT) revenue — the tax travelers pay on hotel stays — which is the legal mechanism that defines both who can receive the money and how funded activities must be framed.

3. The HOT Funding Requirement — The Tourism Nexus
Because every dollar in these programs comes from Hotel Occupancy Tax, every funded activity must demonstrably promote tourism, attract visitors, or contribute to Austin's identity as a cultural destination. This is not optional framing — it is a legal requirement embedded in Texas state law governing how HOT funds can be spent. Applicants who ignore the tourism angle in their applications miss a fundamental scoring and compliance requirement. The website makes this explicit across all six programs: your work must benefit tourists and residents, must be publicly advertised and accessible, and must contribute to Austin's appeal as a place people travel to and spend money in. For arts and heritage organizations, this means every public event, oral history project, and cultural preservation initiative must be described in terms of what it adds to Austin's living cultural identity as experienced by someone who didn't grow up here.

4. Six Programs, Six Different Entry Points
The site describes six distinct ACME programs, each with different eligibility thresholds, award ranges, and applicant types. Elevate ($11M available) is the largest and broadest — it serves individual artists, arts groups, and nonprofits producing public events, requiring two years of creative production history. Thrive is for established nonprofit cultural organizations with five years of operating history and $60K+ annual budgets, offering $85K–$150K per year in multi-year operational support. The Austin Live Music Fund serves professional musicians, music venues, and promoters. The Heritage Preservation Grant funds historic preservation capital projects and heritage events. Nexus serves emerging artists and opens in September 2026. The Creative Space Assistance Program (CSAP) targets commercial creative spaces facing displacement and opens in Summer 2027. Each program has its own scoring rubric, its own eligibility gates, and its own application timeline.

5. The Submittable Portal — The Only Door In
All applications flow through a single portal at thelongcenter.submittable.com/submit. There is no paper application, no email submission, and no alternative path. The site's walkthrough resource is designed to prevent the most common failure modes before applicants even open the portal: creating accounts under the wrong name, selecting the wrong program, failing to verify email, or running into browser incompatibility issues. The site specifically recommends using Chrome with browser auto-complete turned off — not arbitrary advice, but a real technical note because Submittable's form fields can behave unexpectedly with auto-fill enabled. The portal also distinguishes between the "applicant" account (the entity applying) and any fiscal sponsor who may eventually receive the funds — these are different roles in the system and must be set up correctly from the beginning.

6. The Intake Form — First Gate, Not the Application
The first step is an Intake Form, which the walkthrough is careful to describe as something categorically different from the full application. The Intake Form collects basic applicant information and lets the applicant indicate which ACME program(s) they are interested in. Submitting it does not mean you have applied — it triggers the next step. The form can be saved as a draft, returned to, and edited before final submission. The applicant name entered in the Intake Form becomes the official account name for the entire cycle, which means naming conventions matter: the site instructs applicants not to use a project name, but the legal or DBA name of the applicant entity. This single field becomes the identifier on every public-facing document if the grant is awarded.

7. Eligibility Verification — The Four-Day Gate
After the Intake Form is submitted, the system automatically assigns an Eligibility Verification Form — a separate document the applicant must complete to confirm they qualify for the specific program(s) they selected. Applicants only receive eligibility forms for the programs they indicated interest in. This verification step is where operating history, 990 financials, 501(c)(3) determination letters, proof of public events, and geographic documentation are submitted and reviewed. The review window is approximately four business days. If an application does not clear eligibility, the full application never opens. For organizations that might be on the edge of any eligibility threshold — operating history, budget size, mission focus — this is the actual gate that determines whether the rest of the process matters.

8. The Full Application — Invitation Only
Only after passing eligibility verification does an applicant receive an invitation link to the full application for the specific program(s) they qualified for. The full applications are program-specific: Thrive's application, for example, scores 100 points across Creative Work (30), Capacity (40), and Public Connection (30). Each section has word limits (typically 3,000 characters), required document uploads, and specific sub-questions that must each be answered. The site and its companion resources emphasize that the application should be drafted offline first — using the provided Word template — then pasted into Submittable, because the portal does not allow you to recover lost text if a session times out. Late applications are not accepted under any circumstances.

9. Evidence Uploads — One Strong File Beats Several Weak Ones
The walkthrough's guidance on upload evidence is specific and practical: one strong upload per field, maximum three pages, focused on proving exactly the activity you claimed in your answer. The site illustrates this with examples — a flyer that shows the applicant's name, the date with year, and an Austin venue address is strong; a photograph with no identifying information is not. Every upload also requires a brief written explanation (one to three sentences) connecting the document to the specific question. Reviewers are instructed to evaluate based only on what they can verify from the upload and explanation — they do not look up supplemental information, they do not assume familiarity with the applicant's work, and they cannot use context from other parts of the application to fill gaps in a specific upload.

10. Scoring, Panels, and Who Reviews
Applications are reviewed by trained volunteer panels, grouped by artistic discipline and budget size. Thrive uses three panel groups: Exhibition (visual/display-based work), Performance (live or recorded work for audiences), and Arts Education, Arts Service, and Arts Therapy. The highest-scoring applications within each panel group are awarded — the number of awards per group is not equal, meaning competition within a group matters. Panel reviewers are given the scoring rubric in advance and score each application independently before discussing. The site is transparent that final funding recommendations are reviewed and approved by City of Austin leadership after the panel process, meaning there is a second layer of review above the panelists.

11. Compliance Rules That Follow Award
The site and application materials make clear that receiving an ACME grant creates binding obligations beyond the funded period. Awardees must list all public events on VisitAustin.com. They must provide free admission upon request to designated ACME employees and Austin Arts Commission members. They must submit a Final Report and Expense Documentation Form within thirty days of completing their program. Non-compliance with these reporting requirements results in suspension of funds, potential audit, termination of the current contract, and a five-year bar from future ACME funding. The site is explicit: organizations that have failed to comply with a prior grant are flagged as Non-Compliant and are ineligible until the five-year period expires.

12. What This Means for an Applicant Approaching the Deadline
With August 18, 2026 as the hard deadline — late applications not accepted under any circumstances — the walkthrough page is effectively a countdown clock as much as a resource. The process is sequential and has built-in delays: the Intake Form must be submitted, the Eligibility Verification Form must be completed and reviewed (approximately four business days), and only then does the full application open. That means the practical deadline for submitting an Intake Form is closer to August 12–14 to ensure eligibility clears with time to complete the full application. For any organization using a fiscal sponsor, the additional step of preparing and uploading the signed Fiscal Sponsorship Agreement adds time. The website exists to compress that learning curve — but it cannot compress the calendar. Every day spent deciding is a day not spent writing.
