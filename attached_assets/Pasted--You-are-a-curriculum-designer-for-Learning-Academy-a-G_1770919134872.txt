Duration: 20 minutes

Objective: Students will explain why AI makes mistakes (learns from imperfect data, doesn't understand context) and describe when to trust AI versus when to check with humans.

Warm-Up (3 min): Show an autocorrect fail example (appropriate for kids):

Someone types "I'm going to the beach!" → autocorrect changes to "I'm going to the bench!"
Ask: "Why would AI make this mistake? Is it because AI is dumb?"

Direct Instruction (8 min): "Remember: AI learns from information (data). But what happens if that information has mistakes? Or if AI doesn't understand the full picture?"

Reason #1: Garbage In, Garbage Out "AI is like a student who studies from a textbook. If the textbook has wrong information, the student will learn wrong things—even if they study really hard!"

Example: "If AI learns to recognize birds by looking at 1,000 pictures, but all the pictures show birds in cages, AI might think birds are ALWAYS in cages. Then when it sees a bird flying free, it might not recognize it as a bird!"

Reason #2: AI Doesn't Know Context "Context means understanding the whole situation. AI only knows what you tell it, not what you're thinking or feeling."

Example: "If you tell voice assistant, 'Call Mom,' it will call whoever is saved as 'Mom' in your phone—even if your mom is sitting right next to you and you meant to call your grandmother who you also call 'Mom'! A human would understand from the situation, but AI doesn't."

Reason #3: AI Can't Think About Meaning "AI can put words together that sound right, but it doesn't understand if they make sense."

Example story: "Once, an AI was helping write a recipe. It said 'Add 2 cups of salt' when it should have said '2 teaspoons.' AI saw that other recipes said '2 cups of ___' and copied that pattern, but it didn't understand that 2 cups of salt would taste TERRIBLE!"

Face recognition thinking two different people are the same person because they look similar
YouTube recommending a scary video to little kids
GPS sending someone the wrong way because a new road was built
AI chatbot giving wrong answer to a math word problem because it didn't understand the story
Smart speaker ordering 100 dollhouses when a kid said "I want a dollhouse!" on TV
"So what do we do? We don't stop using AI—we just remember it's a TOOL that needs supervision!"

Display poster with 5 W's and explain each:

WHO - Who is this for? Who is involved?

Example: "Tell me about presidents" vs. "Tell me about presidents for a 4th grader"
WHAT - What exactly do you want AI to do?

Example: "Help with math" vs. "Explain how to multiply two-digit numbers"
WHEN - When does this apply? What time period?

Example: "Tell me about technology" vs. "Tell me about technology in the 2020s"
WHERE - Where is this happening? What location matters?

Example: "What's the weather?" vs. "What's the weather in Austin, Texas today?"
WHY/HOW - Why do you need this? How detailed should it be?

Example: "Explain photosynthesis" vs. "Explain photosynthesis simply because I'm writing a children's book"
Demonstration:

Show progression from vague to specific:

Vague Prompt: "Write a story"

AI might write anything—about space, animals, princesses, who knows!
Better Prompt (adds What + Who): "Write a story about a dog for kids"

Now AI knows topic and audience
Even Better (adds When + Where + Why): "Write a short story about a dog who gets lost at a park in Austin, Texas, for 3rd graders learning about being brave"

Now AI has everything it needs to write a great story!
Show actual example outputs if possible (pre-prepared screenshots).

Guided Practice (6 min): "Prompt Building Game"

Give students vague prompts on cards. As a class, improve them using 5 W's:

Card 1: "Tell me about animals"

Who: Is this for me, a younger kid, my teacher?
What: All animals or specific ones? Facts or stories?
When: Animals today or dinosaurs?
Where: Animals in Texas, Africa, oceans?
Why: For a report, just for fun, to compare?
Improved: "Tell me 5 interesting facts about animals in the Texas Hill Country for my 4th-grade science project"

Card 2: "Help me with homework"

(Class works together to improve using 5 W's)
Card 3: "Find a video"

(Class works together to improve using 5 W's)
Create anchor chart: "The 5 W's Make Better Prompts!"

Independent Practice (2 min): Students choose ONE vague prompt from worksheet and improve it using at least 3 of the 5 W's. Write both versions:

Vague: ___
Improved: ___
Closure (1 min): "Quick review! When talking to AI, I should include information about..." (Class calls out the 5 W's)

"Remember: The clearer YOU are, the better AI can help you!"

Assessment:

Formative: Observation during guided practice, checking for understanding of 5 W's
Exit ticket: Before/after prompt comparison on worksheet

Lesson 2: Garbage In, Garbage Out
Duration: 20 minutes

Objective: Students will explain why clear, accurate input leads to useful output, and vague or incorrect input leads to unhelpful responses.

Materials:

"Recipe" demonstration materials (written instructions for making a sandwich)
Example AI conversations showing GIGO principle
Chart paper divided into "Garbage In" and "Gold In" columns
"Prompt Quality Tester" cards
Markers
Warm-Up (3 min): Recipe Following Activity: Show two sets of instructions for making a peanut butter sandwich:

Instructions Set A (Garbage In): "Put stuff on bread. Make a sandwich."

Instructions Set B (Gold In): "Take 2 slices of bread. Spread peanut butter on one slice. Spread jelly on the other slice. Put the slices together with the spread sides facing each other."

Ask: "Which instructions would help you make a better sandwich? Why?"

Act out trying to follow the vague instructions with confusion and humor.

Direct Instruction (7 min): "There's a famous saying in computer science: 'Garbage In, Garbage Out' or GIGO for short!"

Explain GIGO: "What you PUT IN to AI determines what you GET OUT. If you give AI garbage (vague, unclear, wrong information), you'll get garbage out (unhelpful, wrong, or confusing answers). If you give AI gold (clear, specific, accurate information), you'll get gold out (useful, accurate, helpful answers)!"

Visual Representation: Draw simple diagram on board:

[Your Prompt] → [AI Processing] → [AI Response]
     ↓                                    ↓
  Garbage?                            Garbage!
     ↓                                    ↓
   Gold?                               Gold!
Real Examples:

Example 1 - Vague Input:

Garbage In: "How do I do math?"
Garbage Out: AI gives a confusing answer about all of mathematics or asks what you mean
Gold In: "How do I add fractions with different denominators?"
Gold Out: AI gives clear step-by-step instructions
Example 2 - Wrong Information:

Garbage In: "Tell me about the president in 1850" (there were multiple presidents in the 1850s)
Garbage Out: AI might pick one randomly or give wrong information
Gold In: "Who was president in 1850?" OR "Who was president when the Compromise of 1850 was signed?"
Gold Out: AI gives you accurate, specific information
Example 3 - Missing Context:

Garbage In: "Is it good?"
Garbage Out: AI asks "Is what good?" because it doesn't know what you're asking about
Gold In: "Is 'The Lightning Thief' by Rick Riordan a good book for a 4th grader who likes adventure?"
Gold Out: AI gives helpful information about the book and whether it matches your needs
Key Principle: "AI is super smart, but it's not a mind reader! It only knows what you TELL it. So the better information you give, the better help you get!"

Guided Practice (7 min): "Garbage or Gold?" Sorting Game

Create two-column chart: "Garbage In" | "Gold In"

Show prompt cards one at a time. Class discusses: Is this garbage or gold? Why? How could we make garbage prompts better?

Prompt Cards:

"Tell me stuff about space"
Garbage! → Improve: "Tell me 3 facts about Mars for my 3rd-grade science report"
"Explain photosynthesis in simple terms for a 9-year-old"
Gold! (specific, includes audience, clear request)
"Help"
Garbage! → Improve: "Help me understand how to subtract three-digit numbers with regrouping"
"What are fun games for kids who like building things and solving puzzles?"
Gold! (specific interests, clear question)
"Write something"
Garbage! → Improve: "Write a 5-sentence paragraph about my summer vacation to South Padre Island"
"Find info"
Garbage! → Improve: "Find information about Abraham Lincoln's childhood for my history project"
"Create a list of 5 healthy breakfast ideas that are quick to make"
Gold! (specific number, topic, constraint)
"Do my homework"
Garbage! (Also, using AI to DO homework is not okay—we use AI to LEARN!)
For each "Garbage" prompt, have class help improve it using 5 W's from Lesson 1.

Independent Practice (2 min): Students draw or write two examples:

One "Garbage In" prompt and what might go wrong
One "Gold In" prompt and why it's good
Closure (1 min): Class chant: "Garbage in, garbage out! Gold in, gold out! Be specific and clear, and AI will help you out!"

Post new rule on classroom AI guidelines: "Give clear, specific prompts!"

Assessment:

Formative: Participation in sorting game, reasoning provided
Exit ticket: Student-created examples showing understanding

Lesson 3: Adding Context and Details
Duration: 20 minutes

Objective: Students will improve prompts by adding relevant context, details, and examples, demonstrating iterative prompt refinement.

Materials:

"Before and After" prompt comparison posters
"Context Clues" cards
Iterative prompt improvement worksheet
Example AI interactions showing iteration
Chart paper for collaborative improvement activity
Warm-Up (4 min): "20 Questions" Game - Modified

Teacher thinks of an object. Students ask yes/no questions to guess it.

After 5-7 questions: "Notice how each question gives you more CONTEXT about what I'm thinking of? That's exactly how you improve prompts for AI!"

Direct Instruction (7 min): "Today we're learning to be 'Prompt Improvers'—people who make their prompts better and better by adding helpful details!"

What is Context? "Context is all the information around something that helps you understand it better."

Example:

Without context: "It's cold."
With context: "It's cold outside in December in Minnesota, so I need a heavy winter coat."
The context (December, Minnesota, outside) helps us understand HOW cold and WHAT to do about it!

Why AI Needs Context:

Show side-by-side comparison:

Without Context:

Prompt: "Give me a recipe"
AI Response: Could give you a recipe for anything—cookies, salad, chicken soup, slime!
Problem: AI doesn't know what you need!
With Context:

Prompt: "Give me a simple recipe for chocolate chip cookies that a 9-year-old can help make, using ingredients most people have at home"
AI Response: Gives you an easy, kid-friendly recipe!
Success: AI knows exactly what you need!
Types of Helpful Context:

Your Background: "I'm a 4th grader" or "I've never done this before" or "I already know basic addition"
Your Goal: "I need this for a school project" or "I want to learn for fun" or "I'm trying to help my younger sibling"
Constraints/Limits: "Keep it under 5 sentences" or "Use only simple words" or "I have 15 minutes"
Examples: "Like the book 'Charlotte's Web'" or "Similar to a golden retriever but smaller"
Preferences: "I like science fiction" or "I don't like spicy food" or "Make it funny"
Demonstration - Iterative Improvement:

Show how a prompt gets better with each iteration:

Version 1 (Basic): "Tell me about dogs"

Version 2 (Add Who - Audience): "Tell me about dogs for a 4th grader"

Version 3 (Add What - Specific Info): "Tell me about what dogs eat for a 4th grader's science report"

Version 4 (Add Why - Purpose + Constraint): "Explain what dogs eat and why it's important for a 4th grader's science report, using 3-5 sentences"

Final Gold Prompt!

Key Insight: "You don't have to make the perfect prompt the first time! You can keep adding details to make it better, just like revising your writing!"

Guided Practice (6 min): "Pump Up the Prompt" Activity

Work as class to improve basic prompts by adding context:

Prompt 1: "Tell me about weather"

Add WHO: "for a 3rd grader"
Add WHAT: "about how clouds form"
Add WHY: "for my science homework"
Add CONSTRAINT: "in a short paragraph I can understand"
FINAL: "Explain how clouds form for a 3rd grader's science homework in a short, easy-to-understand paragraph"
Prompt 2: "Find a game"

Students suggest what context to add
Build it together step by step
Compare first and final versions
Prompt 3: "Help me write a story"

Students suggest context additions
Build collaboratively
Show how each addition makes AI's job easier
Create visual "Prompt Pump-Up" poster showing progression.

Independent Practice (2 min): Students complete worksheet:

Choose ONE basic prompt from list:

"Tell me about animals"
"Find a video"
"Help me with math"
"Make a list"
Improve it THREE times, adding different types of context each time:

First improvement: ___
Second improvement: ___
Third improvement: ___
Closure (1 min): "Remember: Prompts are like rough drafts—you can always make them better! Add context, add details, add examples. The more information you give AI, the better it can help you!"

Quick share: 1-2 students share their prompt progression.

Assessment:

Formative: Observation during collaborative improvement, checking for understanding of context types
Exit ticket: Worksheet showing iterative prompt improvement

Lesson 4: Polite Communication with AI
Duration: 20 minutes

Objective: Students will demonstrate polite communication practices when interacting with AI, understanding that good habits shape character regardless of whether AI has feelings.

Materials:

Polite vs. rude prompt comparison cards
"Golden Words" poster (please, thank you, excuse me)
Role-play scenario cards
"AI Manners Guide" template for class creation
Chart paper
Markers
Warm-Up (3 min): Manners Check-In: "Think-Pair-Share: When should you use polite words like 'please' and 'thank you'? Why do we use them?"

Students share with partner, then a few share with class.

Collect responses: with parents, teachers, friends, store workers, etc.

Direct Instruction (7 min): "But wait! Does AI have feelings?"

"Great question! AI does NOT have feelings. It can't feel happy when you're polite or sad when you're rude. So why does being polite to AI matter?"

Three Big Reasons to Be Polite with AI:

Reason #1: Building Good Habits "Everything you practice becomes a habit. If you practice being rude to AI, those rude habits might come out when you talk to real people—and THEY do have feelings!"

Example:

If you get used to typing "Give me answers now!" to AI, you might accidentally text that to your teacher!
If you practice saying "Please help me understand this," that respectful habit helps you everywhere!
Analogy: "It's like practicing a sport. You don't just play nice during the game—you practice being a good sport all the time so it becomes automatic!"

Reason #2: Getting Better Results "Guess what? Being specific AND polite actually helps AI understand you better!"

Show comparison:

Rude: "Tell me stuff"
Polite: "Could you please explain photosynthesis in simple terms?"
"The polite version is ALSO clearer! Manners and good communication go together!"

Reason #3: Showing Respect for People Who Made AI "Real people—engineers, designers, programmers—worked hard to create AI tools to help us. When we use AI respectfully, we're honoring their work!"

What Do Polite Prompts Look Like?

Polite Prompt Formula:

Start friendly: "Hi!" or "Hello" (optional but nice)
Use polite words: "Please," "Could you," "Would you mind"
Be clear about what you need: (use 5 W's!)
Say thanks: "Thank you!" or "Thanks for your help!"
Examples:

Rude Prompt: "Do my homework"

Problems: Demanding, unclear, asking AI to cheat
Polite Prompt: "Hi! Could you please help me understand how to solve this math problem? I'm working on fractions with different denominators. Thank you!"

Better: Friendly, clear, asking for HELP (not answers), grateful
Rude Prompt: "Find me a video now"

Problems: Demanding, no details
Polite Prompt: "Hello! Would you please help me find an educational video about the water cycle for kids my age? I need it for my science project. Thanks!"

Better: Friendly, specific, clear purpose
Important Note About Cheating: "Remember: Being polite is different from asking AI to do your work for you!

❌ Don't ask: 'Write my essay for me'
✅ Do ask: 'Can you please help me brainstorm ideas for my essay about my favorite book?'
AI should help you LEARN, not do the learning for you!"

Guided Practice (7 min): Role-Play: Polite vs. Rude Requests

Divide class into pairs. Give each pair a scenario card.

Scenario Cards:

You need help with spelling words
You want to find information about space
You need a recipe suggestion
You want help understanding your math homework
You're looking for a book recommendation
You need facts about animals for a report
Activity:

First, one partner role-plays AI, the other makes a RUDE request
Class discusses: What made it rude? How did it feel?
Then, same partners switch and demonstrate a POLITE request
Class discusses: What made it polite? How did it feel different?
After 2-3 pairs demonstrate, create class anchor chart:

"Our Class AI Manners Guide"

DO:

✅ Use "please" and "thank you"
✅ Be specific and clear
✅ Ask for help learning, not help cheating
✅ Treat AI like you'd treat a helpful friend
✅ Use complete sentences when you can
DON'T:

❌ Demand or command rudely
❌ Use mean words (even though AI can't feel them—practice good habits!)
❌ Ask AI to do your work for you
❌ Be vague and expect AI to read your mind
❌ Forget that your habits matter
Independent Practice (2 min): Students rewrite ONE rude prompt to make it polite:

Choose from:

"Tell me about Texas"
"Find stuff about dogs"
"Do my math"
"Give me answers"
Rewrite as: "Polite version: ___"

Closure (1 min): "Let's make a promise together!"

Class repeats: "I promise to practice good manners with AI and with people, because my habits matter and I'm building the person I want to be!"

Post the "Class AI Manners Guide" in classroom.

Assessment:

Formative: Observation during role-play, participation in creating class guide
Exit ticket: Rewritten polite prompt showing understanding

How does your family practice polite communication?
Do you use "please" and "thank you" with voice assistants? Why or why not?
Practice together: Ask Alexa/Siri/Google Assistant something politely!

Pre-teach vocabulary: context, specific, polite, iteration
Provide word bank of polite phrases for reference
Use more oral practice before written work
On Grade Level:

Standard instruction as outlined
Mix of guided and independent practice
Balance scaffolding with challenge
Encourage peer collaboration and discussion
Above Grade Level:

Challenge: "Create a prompt that includes ALL 5 W's AND context in one sentence"
Extension: Research how AI chatbots use prompts differently than search engines

Leadership: Serve as "Prompt Coaches" helping classmates improve their prompts

English Language Learners:

Provide 5 W's visual cards with images and labels in home language

Allow use of translation tools for complex prompts
Partner with bilingual buddies
Model prompts with think-aloud in simple English
Provide examples of polite phrases in multiple languages
Extra processing time before calling on ELL students
Accept prompts in home language during practice phase
Students with IEPs:

Extended time for all activities
Reduce writing load: oral prompts accepted, scribe support available
Provide graphic organizers for 5 W's framework
Use color-coding for different parts of prompts (Who = blue, What = red, etc.)
Break multi-step tasks into individual steps with check-ins
Offer movement breaks between lessons

Allow alternative response modes (typed, voice recorded, illustrated)
Simplify language in instructions
Provide worked examples as models

Summative Assessment: End-of-module performance task: "Prompt Expert Challenge"

Task Description: Students receive a scenario that requires AI assistance. They must create and document their prompt-building process.

Scenario Options (student chooses one):

You need to learn about the Texas Revolution for a history project
You want to find a new book to read that's similar to your favorite book
You need help understanding how to multiply fractions
You're planning a science experiment and need ideas
Requirements: Students must submit:

First Draft Prompt - Initial attempt (can be basic)
Analysis - What's missing? What could be clearer? (Use 5 W's checklist)
Improved Prompt - Second version with added context/details
Final Prompt - Polished version that is clear, specific, AND polite
Reflection - Brief explanation: "My final prompt is good because..."
Rubric (4 levels):

Criteria	Beginning (1)	Developing (2)	Proficient (3)	Advanced (4)
Use of 5 W's	Includes 0-1 W's	Includes 2 W's	Includes 3-4 W's appropriately	Includes all 5 W's naturally and effectively
Specificity	Very vague, unclear what's needed	Somewhat specific, some clarity	Clear and specific request	Exceptionally clear with relevant details
Context & Details	No context provided	Minimal context	Appropriate context and helpful details	Rich context that guides AI perfectly
Polite Communication	Rude or demanding tone	Missing polite words	Includes "please/thank you"	Consistently polite throughout
Understanding GIGO	No evidence of improvement	Minimal improvement shown	Clear improvement from first to final draft	Sophisticated improvement with analysis
Reflection Quality	Incomplete or unclear	Basic explanation	Clear explanation of improvements	Insightful reflection showing deep understanding
Scoring:

Beginning: 6-9 points (needs additional support)
Developing: 10-13 points (approaching standard)
Proficient: 14-17 points (meets standard)
Advanced: 18-24 points (exceeds standard)
