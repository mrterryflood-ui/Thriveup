## LOCATION PROFILE — AUSTIN / TRAVIS COUNTY, TEXAS

Austin was founded in 1839 as the capital of the Republic of Texas and developed first as a government and university center, then as a technology and service economy anchored by state government, the University of Texas, semiconductor manufacturing, and later software and creative industries. Travis County, established in 1840, shifted over decades from a smaller regional government/agricultural base to one of the fastest-growing metropolitan counties in the country. That growth created a familiar split-screen reality: regional prosperity and high-skill job expansion on one hand, and deep affordability strain, displacement, childcare shortages, and unequal access to health, transportation, and economic mobility on the other. East Austin in particular carries the legacy of segregation, disinvestment, and displacement pressure that continues to shape service demand and funding narratives around equity, access, and culturally competent family support.

Demographically, Travis County now exceeds 1.3 million residents, with the City of Austin near 975,000–980,000. Median household income is roughly in the low-to-mid $90,000s for Austin and lower countywide depending on ACS year, but those topline figures mask major neighborhood-level disparities. Poverty remains around the low teens countywide, with child poverty materially higher in many service areas. The population is racially and ethnically diverse: large White non-Hispanic and Hispanic/Latino populations, substantial Black/African American and Asian communities, and many mixed-status and multilingual households. Educational attainment is high overall because of the metro’s professional base, but not evenly distributed. Housing cost burden is one of the region’s defining stressors: renters face severe rent pressure, ownership has become less attainable, and family-serving nonprofits are operating in a market where housing and childcare together can block workforce participation. For Child Inc., that matters because many federal and state programs reward integrated family economic stabilization, workforce participation supports, and service delivery in disadvantaged census tracts.

Infrastructure and social conditions reinforce that case. Austin has stronger institutional capacity than many regions, but rapid growth has strained transportation, childcare supply, housing stock affordability, and social-service access. Transit exists but is uneven as a practical option for many low-income working families, especially for shift workers. Broadband access is better than rural Texas but affordability and device access gaps still affect family engagement, telehealth, and workforce training. Public health needs include maternal/child health disparities, behavioral health access gaps, food insecurity in specific neighborhoods, and continued pressure on school readiness systems. Safety-net demand remains elevated where housing instability, wage stagnation at the lower end of the labor market, and family stress intersect. Strategically, this profile triggers strong relevance for HHS/ACF, HRSA, SAMHSA, DOL, USDA nutrition/community food, HUD self-sufficiency and homelessness-related family supports, ED community-school and literacy pathways, and city/county human-services contracts tied to equity and family stabilization.
