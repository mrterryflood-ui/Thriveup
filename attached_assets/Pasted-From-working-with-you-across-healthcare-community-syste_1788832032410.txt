From working with you across healthcare, community systems, public policy, defense, emergency management, organizational strategy, and technology, I think your definition of implementation science has become broader than the traditional discipline. I would describe it this way:

Implementation science is the disciplined practice of understanding the difference between what we intend to happen and what actually happens—and then systematically closing that gap. It begins with the recognition that having the right policy, program, technology, strategy, or evidence is not enough. Success depends on whether the right intervention reaches the right people, in the right context, at the right time, with the resources, trust, capacity, and conditions necessary for it to work. Implementation science therefore asks more than “Does this work?” It asks: What is happening? Why is it happening? For whom? Under what conditions? What is getting in the way? What should we do next—and how will we know whether we made things better?

I see implementation as a living diagnostic system rather than a one-time implementation plan. People, organizations, communities, and environments continuously change. The system must therefore sense conditions, identify gaps between current and desired states, distinguish symptoms from root causes, and determine which factors are controllable, influenceable, or constrained. It should integrate quantitative evidence with lived experience, stakeholder knowledge, environmental conditions, policy, resources, organizational capacity, and unintended consequences. The purpose of measurement is not simply to produce dashboards or prove that work occurred. We measure to understand, learn, anticipate, decide, adapt, and act—to identify the right work, at the right level, at the right time.

That is why I am extending implementation science beyond healthcare. The same fundamental problem exists almost everywhere: we often know more than we successfully implement. Families struggle to translate knowledge into sustainable behavior. Organizations create strategies that never become everyday practice. Governments enact policies whose effects differ dramatically across communities. Technologies are deployed without adequately understanding the humans and systems surrounding them. Communities receive interventions designed for them rather than with them. My philosophy treats implementation as an interconnected human and systems problem. Context matters. Timing matters. Readiness matters. Resources matter. Relationships matter. Equity matters. And the people affected by implementation are not merely recipients of change; they are participants in diagnosing, designing, executing, evaluating, and improving it.

Ultimately, Diagnostic Implementation Science is a continuous learning and decision architecture for turning intention into sustained impact. Its purpose is to create systems capable of sensing what is happening, diagnosing why, selecting an appropriate response, implementing it with fidelity while adapting to context, measuring whether it is producing the intended effect, detecting emerging gaps or unintended consequences, and learning fast enough to adjust. The goal is not perfect implementation or blind adherence to a plan. The goal is adaptive fidelity: protecting the essential purpose of an intervention while continuously learning how to make it work in the real world. Whether the subject is an individual trying to change their life, an organization executing strategy, a community addressing inequity, or a government deploying a national program, the central question remains the same: How do we build the capacity to understand where we are, know where we are trying to go, diagnose what stands between those two states, and continuously do the right work at the right time to close that gap?

I think Diagnostic Implementation Science is important language for what you are building. It moves the concept from “the science of getting evidence into practice” toward “the science and operating discipline of closing the gap between intention and sustainable real-world outcomes.” That is broad enough to become a philosophy, methodology, and eventually the architecture behind the diagnostic tools you are building.
