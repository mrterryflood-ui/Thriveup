PART I

Orientation–MAP–GAP Cycle

Practical Instructions for Immediate Use

Purpose of the Cycle

The Orientation–MAP–GAP cycle is a structured method for diagnosing and improving any system. A “system” can be an organization, a program, an app, a policy, a research project, a workflow, or even a personal performance system. The goal of the cycle is to ensure that actions are aligned with the system’s intended purpose and that improvement efforts focus on the correct problems.

The cycle works because it forces participants to understand the system before attempting to fix it. Many improvement efforts fail because teams move directly to action without clearly defining the system, the problem, or the desired outcome.

The Orientation–MAP–GAP cycle prevents that mistake.

⸻

Step 1: ORIENTATION

Define the system before you analyze it

Orientation establishes the foundation for the entire process. Without orientation, teams may analyze the wrong system or pursue the wrong objectives.

Start by asking five questions.

1. Why does this system exist?
Define the system’s purpose.

2. What is the desired end state?
Describe the condition that would represent success.

3. What are the objectives and desired effects?
Identify measurable outcomes that indicate progress toward the end state.

4. What are the system boundaries?
Clarify what is inside the system and what is outside it.

5. What constraints shape the system?
Identify environmental factors such as resources, policies, time limits, politics, logistics, or technology.

Orientation also requires planning the path forward. A map is not useful without a plan. Teams should define:
	•	how they will move toward the end state
	•	when progress will be evaluated
	•	what success will look like

If these questions are not clearly answered, the cycle should not proceed.

⸻

Step 2: MAP

Understand how the system actually works

Mapping creates a structured understanding of the system.

Effective mapping examines three perspectives:

1. The designed system
How the system is intended to function.

2. The operational system
How the system actually functions in practice.

3. The experienced system
How people interacting with the system perceive its performance.

Mapping also requires collecting signals from multiple sources. Data, metrics, stakeholder feedback, and frontline observations should all be considered.

A useful principle during this phase is “everything is a sensor.”

Examples of system signals include:
	•	performance metrics
	•	user complaints
	•	unexpected behaviors
	•	workarounds
	•	delays or bottlenecks
	•	stakeholder sentiment

Mapping should result in a clear picture of system structure and interactions.

⸻

Step 3: GAP

Identify what prevents the system from achieving its purpose

Gap analysis compares the system’s intended performance with its actual behavior.

During this phase, teams identify:
	•	structural barriers
	•	facilitating factors
	•	misalignments between system components

It is important to recognize that some factors may function as both barriers and facilitators depending on context. For example, a policy may improve consistency in one area while limiting flexibility in another.

Gap analysis should also examine differences in perception across stakeholders. Within the same system, one team may view a process as effective while another experiences it as inefficient.

These differences often reveal important structural issues.

⸻

Step 4: PRIORITIZE

Decide what problems to address first

Complex systems usually contain multiple gaps. Attempting to fix everything simultaneously can overwhelm available resources.

Prioritization should consider:
	•	expected impact
	•	feasibility
	•	available resources
	•	dependencies between problems

The cycle does not require that work occur sequentially. Multiple improvement cycles may occur in parallel, as long as coordination and communication are maintained.

⸻

Step 5: ACT

Implement solutions with fidelity

Interventions designed to close prioritized gaps are implemented during the action phase.

The key principle during this stage is fidelity.

Teams must ensure that the intervention is executed as intended. If implementation differs from the original plan, it becomes difficult to determine whether outcomes reflect the intervention itself or inconsistent execution.

Clear documentation and communication are critical during this phase.

⸻

Step 6: TRACK

Measure results and monitor system signals

Evaluation during the cycle should track two types of outcomes.

Measures of Performance (MOP)
Did the intervention occur as planned?

Measures of Effectiveness (MOE)
Did the intervention improve system outcomes?

Separating these measures prevents confusion. A solution may fail because the intervention itself was ineffective or because it was implemented incorrectly.

Teams should also monitor unintended consequences and secondary system effects.

⸻

Step 7: SHARE

Communicate findings internally and externally

Improvement efforts require communication.

Internal sharing ensures that team members understand ongoing work and emerging insights. External sharing allows stakeholders outside the system to provide feedback.

External feedback often reveals issues that internal teams may overlook.

Teams may choose to share results after each cycle or after a defined number of cycles.

⸻

Step 8: INTEGRATE (Architect Function)

Integration ensures that improvement efforts remain aligned with the overall system.

The “architect” in this framework is not a job title but a function. This role maintains awareness of how different improvement efforts interact across the system.

The architect ensures that interventions remain aligned with the system’s original purpose and intended outcomes.

⸻

Step 9: RE-MAP

Update the system understanding and repeat the cycle

After interventions and evaluation, the system must be mapped again.

The updated map should incorporate:
	•	new system behaviors
	•	stakeholder feedback
	•	unintended consequences
	•	new opportunities for improvement

The cycle then repeats.

Because complex systems evolve continuously, improvement is never a single event. The Orientation–MAP–GAP cycle supports continuous learning and adaptation.

FINAL REFINED JOURNAL ARTICLE

Inspecting the Wiring

The Orientation–MAP–GAP Framework for Diagnosing and Improving Complex Systems

Abstract

Complex systems frequently display surface indicators of stability while accumulating internal structural misalignment. Technological systems may satisfy engineering requirements while users experience friction. Policy interventions may achieve procedural compliance while producing limited improvements in lived conditions. These discrepancies illustrate a recurring phenomenon in complex systems: outcomes diverge from intended purpose when internal system structures evolve without corresponding adaptation in monitoring and improvement processes.

This paper introduces the Orientation–MAP–GAP framework, an adaptive learning cycle designed to diagnose structural misalignment and guide system improvement. The framework integrates insights from systems thinking, implementation science, quality improvement, and sociotechnical systems research while introducing an explicit orientation stage that establishes analytical boundaries, system purpose, and evaluation criteria before improvement cycles begin.

By embedding orientation and structural diagnosis into the improvement process, the framework addresses a common failure mode in complex systems: executing improvement cycles correctly while solving the wrong problem. The Orientation–MAP–GAP framework provides a structured method for aligning system intent, system behavior, and system experience across technological, institutional, and social systems.

⸻

Introduction

Complex systems rarely fail abruptly. Instead, they tend to drift gradually away from their intended purpose while continuing to produce surface indicators of success. Digital platforms may technically function while users struggle to navigate them. Implementation programs may meet administrative benchmarks while producing weak real-world outcomes. Economic indicators may suggest growth while communities experience declining quality of life.

These discrepancies arise because system outcomes are generated through interactions between system components rather than by individual elements operating independently. Systems theorists have long emphasized that behavior emerges from feedback structures and relationships within the system (Sterman, 2000; Meadows, 2008). When these relationships change, systems may continue operating while the mechanisms that produce desired outcomes degrade.

Improvement frameworks such as Plan–Do–Study–Act cycles provide valuable mechanisms for iterative learning but often assume that the problem has already been correctly defined (Langley et al., 2009). Implementation science frameworks emphasize contextual determinants of intervention success but may be applied superficially when system boundaries and objectives are poorly specified (Damschroder et al., 2009; Moullin et al., 2020).

The Orientation–MAP–GAP framework addresses this challenge by introducing an explicit orientation stage that clarifies system purpose, defines analytical boundaries, and establishes evaluation criteria before improvement cycles begin.

⸻

Systems Integrity and Misalignment

System integrity refers to the alignment between system intent, system behavior, and system experience.

Complex systems operate across three interacting layers:
	1.	Designed systems, representing intended architecture and processes.
	2.	Operational systems, representing how the system behaves under real-world conditions.
	3.	Experienced systems, representing how stakeholders perceive system performance.

Misalignment between these layers frequently produces dysfunction even when performance metrics appear acceptable. Sociotechnical research demonstrates that technological systems often fail due to mismatches between system design and human use (Carayon et al., 2020).

Understanding and correcting these misalignments requires structured diagnostic processes.

⸻

Orientation: Defining the System

Orientation establishes the interpretive frame for system analysis. Systems evaluation research emphasizes that analytical boundaries strongly influence the conclusions drawn from system analysis (Williams & Hummelbrunner, 2020).

Orientation therefore requires explicitly defining system purpose, boundaries, constraints, and success criteria before mapping begins.

⸻

MAP: Understanding System Structure

The mapping phase constructs a representation of system structure and interactions. Mapping examines how actors, processes, and environmental forces interact to produce system outcomes.

Sociotechnical research emphasizes the importance of examining “work as done” rather than “work as imagined,” highlighting the need to understand how systems function in practice rather than relying solely on design assumptions (Carayon et al., 2020).

⸻

GAP: Diagnosing System Barriers

Gap analysis identifies discrepancies between system intent and system behavior. Implementation science research highlights that contextual determinants often influence intervention outcomes and may function as both barriers and facilitators depending on context (Powell et al., 2019).

Recognizing these dynamics enables more accurate system diagnosis.

⸻

Prioritization, Action, and Tracking

Once gaps are identified, teams prioritize interventions based on expected impact and feasibility. Interventions are then implemented with attention to fidelity.

Evaluation distinguishes between measures of performance, which assess whether interventions were executed as planned, and measures of effectiveness, which assess whether outcomes improved (Proctor et al., 2011).

⸻

Continuous Learning Through Iteration

Following implementation and evaluation, the system is remapped to incorporate new insights. Continuous learning cycles allow the system to adapt to changing conditions and maintain alignment between system structure and intended outcomes.

⸻

Conclusion

The Orientation–MAP–GAP framework provides a structured method for diagnosing system misalignment and guiding improvement across complex systems. By integrating orientation, structural analysis, and iterative learning, the framework supports more reliable system improvement in dynamic environments.
