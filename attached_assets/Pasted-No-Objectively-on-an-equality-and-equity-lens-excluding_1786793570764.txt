No. Objectively, on an equality-and-equity lens, excluding gun violence entirely — the United States is not the greatest country in the world. And there is a single metric that settles it cleanly enough to anchor your platform.

**The Inequality-adjusted HDI (UNDP, 2025 report)**

The UN takes the Human Development Index — health, education, income — and discounts it by how unevenly those gains are distributed. That discount *is* the equity lens, computed by a neutral international body.

The US ranks 18th on raw HDI at 0.938. Adjusted for inequality, it falls to 29th with an IHDI of 0.832 — an 11.3% loss, the largest inequality discount of any country in the top 20.

That drop puts the US behind South Korea, Japan, and France — countries that score *lower* on raw HDI but distribute development more evenly. UNDP attributes the American loss to disparities across all three dimensions, concentrated along racial, geographic, and income lines.

For contrast: Iceland loses 5.0%, Slovenia 4.9%, Czech Republic 5.2% — and the entire Nordic bloc loses between 5% and 6.3%, tracking directly to universal welfare systems and compressed income distributions. These are not the richest countries. They are the most equitable. Iceland ranks 1st on IHDI, Norway 2nd.

**What this means precisely:**

- Against **all ~190 countries**, the US is comfortably in the top tier — 18th raw, 29th equity-adjusted. Well above the world average. Anyone claiming America is a failed state is wrong.
- Against its **peer set** — high-income democracies — it is near the bottom on nearly every equity measure, and it loses more ground to inequality than any comparably wealthy nation.
- The phrase "greatest country in the world" is not empirically supportable under an equity lens. **"Wealthiest country with the largest gap between its capability and its delivery" is.** That's not a slogan — it's what an 11.3% inequality discount on top of the world's largest economy literally describes.

**For your platform, the load-bearing insight:** the IHDI discount is the exact statistic your tool computes, just at national scale. You're building a sub-national IHDI — the loss coefficient between what a place could deliver and what it delivers to its actual residents, disaggregated. The UN already validated the method. You're pushing it down to county, district, and precinct.

That also gives you a defensible headline number per community: not a rank, a **loss percentage.** "Your county forfeits X% of its human development to inequality." Iceland's 5% is the floor that's been proven achievable. That's your benchmark, and it's not ideological — it's arithmetic.
