**Economic mobility — the "American Dream" gap**
- Chetty's landmark study found the chance of achieving upward mobility (bottom 20% of income → top 20%) is nearly twice as high in Canada as in the US.
- Among 24 comparable middle- and high-income countries, the US ranks 16th in intergenerational earnings mobility.
- Absolute mobility has collapsed generationally: over 90% of Americans born in 1940 out-earned their parents by age 30; only about 50% of those born in 1980 can say the same — and US economic inequality is higher than virtually any other country at its income level, which is the key driver of that decline.
- Your instinct is confirmed by the data itself: mobility remains "insufficiently accessible to some," and the strongest predictors of low mobility are racial segregation, income inequality, school quality, family structure, and social capital — i.e., access and geography, not effort or "opportunity" in the abstract.

**Deaths of despair (drugs, alcohol, suicide) — a direct measure of who's being left behind**
- The US "deaths of despair" mortality rate more than doubled from 28.6 per 100,000 in 1999 to 71.99 per 100,000 in 2023 — nearly 200,000 deaths a year.
- A direct US/UK/Canada comparison found the US uniquely stands out for the size of its increase in drug-related mortality among peer English-speaking nations.
- In 2023, drug overdoses killed 338.6% more Americans than homicide and 103% more than suicide — over 105,000 deaths in one year alone.
- Overdose deaths fell sharply in 2024 (down ~24% nationally), but Black and Native American communities saw rates 1.5x the national average even after the decline — the improvement itself is unequal.

You named it correctly: what these numbers show isn't "some have a lot, some have a little" as a natural or acceptable spread — it's that the US produces first-world outcomes for people with access (insurance, zip code, school district, family wealth) and third-world or conflict-zone outcomes for people without it, inside the same national economy. That gap — not the national average — is the actual policy signal, and it's the variable your D-in-D tool should be built around: access-to-X as the independent variable, outcome-Y as the dependent one, measured state-by-state and subgroup-by-subgroup against the same metric in peer nations.
