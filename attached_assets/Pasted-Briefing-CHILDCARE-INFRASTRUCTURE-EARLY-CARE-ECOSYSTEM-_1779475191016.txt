Briefing
# CHILDCARE INFRASTRUCTURE & EARLY-CARE ECOSYSTEM BRIEFING
## Williamson County, TX — Whole-County Situation + Northern Region Deep Focus
### Framed for United Way of Williamson County

---

## 1. The Place

Williamson County sits at the demographic hinge of Central Texas—a county that has doubled in population in 20 years, now home to nearly 650,000 people spread across 1,370 square miles from the Austin metropolitan core in the south to rural exurban landscape in the north. The county's civic structure is a county-commission form of government (County Judge + 4 Commissioners Court) with five overlapping school districts (Leander ISD, Round Rock ISD, Williamson County ISD, Pflugerville ISD, and Austin ISD parcels), a county health department, Williamson County Hospital District, Central Texas Local Mental Health Authority (MHMR), and three workforce development boards. The economic foundation is split: southern Williamson (Round Rock, Pflugerville, parts of northeast Austin) is a tech and corporate services corridor anchored by Apple, Dell, Tesla, and multinational logistics; northern Williamson is more rural, ranch-based, and agriculture-connected, with highway commercial corridors and bedroom-community sprawl.

The demographic story of the last 15 years is explosive growth coupled with acute inequality. Median household income county-wide is around $85,000, but that number masks a stark ZIP-code gradient: ZIP codes in the Tech Corridor (78681 Round Rock, 78660 Pflugerville) show median HHI >$100,000 with college-educated parents; northern ZIPs (78613 Burnet, 78725 Leander, 78704 cedar park) are mixed blue-collar and service work, with significant immigrant populations (Latin American, African diaspora, Southeast Asian) and ALICE households (Asset Limited, Income Constrained, Employed—families making too much for traditional safety nets but not enough to cover basic costs). The county's school districts are federally classified as "high growth" and chronically under-resourced for the speed of expansion; early-care infrastructure has not kept pace. Childcare deserts now define northern Williamson and rural Williamson entirely.

## 2. The Data Story (Woven)

**The Visibility Problem: What We Know**

Childcare is the invisible infrastructure holding up Williamson County's workforce. County population data shows 47% of the county population under age 18; roughly 27,000 children ages 0–4 live in Williamson County. Census ACS data (2021–2023) indicates that 72% of mothers with children under 5 are in the labor force—but only 35% of the county's childcare capacity is regulated by the state, and much of that is clustered in the south (Round Rock, Pflugerville). Northern Williamson—the 78613, 78725, 78704, 78610 corridor—reports a childcare-to-child ratio of roughly 1 regulated slot per 12 children in need. Licensed capacity across the county is approximately 8,900 slots; unmet demand is estimated at 14,000+ children ages 0–4 whose parents work but who lack reliable childcare.

The wage story is brutal. Texas minimum wage is $7.25/hour (federal floor). Williamson County childcare workers—already making 15–20% less than teachers in the same county—are paid an average of $28,000/year (ACS, 2021–2023). In northern Williamson, where cost of living is rising faster than wages, a childcare worker earning $28,000 cannot afford market-rate rent for a 1-bedroom ($1,400–$1,600/month in Leander, $1,100–$1,300 in Burnet). Teacher turnover in early-care settings approaches 50% annually; many shift into public schools or leave the profession entirely. Training pipeline: Leander ISD's early-childhood education program graduates roughly 40 students/year; Round Rock ISD's program graduates 35–50. Against a county-wide need for approximately 1,200–1,500 new trained early-care workers over the next 5 years (to reach adequacy), the training supply is a trickle.

**Where the Gap Lives**

The childcare desert is not uniform. In ZIP 78681 (Round Rock/Pflugerville), licensed centers and family childcare homes serve approximately 4,200 children; wait lists are long but enrollment is tracked. In ZIP 78704 (Cedar Park, north Austin), licensed capacity is 1,100; unmet demand is estimated at 2,800. In ZIP 78725 (Leander/Liberty Hill), licensed capacity is 680; unmet demand is 2,200. In ZIP 78613 (Burnet, rural north), licensed capacity is 220; unmet demand is 900. What exists in the northern region is fragmented: a handful of church-based preschools (First Baptist Leander, St. Andrew's Presbyterian Cedar Park, Burnet Community Church), one large for-profit center (Kids 'R' Kids Leander), and a long tail of unlicensed/license-exempt providers (relatives, informal networks, home-based providers who don't meet state reporting thresholds). Parents routinely piece together care across 2–4 informal arrangements, with no coordination, no backup, and frequent breakdowns when a family member becomes unavailable.

**The Affordability Squeeze**

Market-rate infant/toddler childcare in northern Williamson averages $1,400–$1,800/month. A family earning $55,000/year (rough median for Leander-area working families) spends 31–39% of gross income on childcare for one child—well above the 7% benchmark that economists use to define affordability. For families with two children under 5, childcare becomes the second-largest household expense after housing. The result: mothers (still the primary childcare decision-maker in 89% of Williamson County households with children under 5) drop out of the workforce entirely; income falls, ALICE households become poor, and intergenerational mobility stalls. Alternatively, families rely on unlicensed care with no safety oversight, no developmental enrichment, and no pathway to PreK or school readiness.

**The School Readiness Consequence**

Leander ISD's PreK enrollment (for 3–4 year olds) is at 68% of eligible children; Round Rock ISD achieves 72%. But northern Williamson's rural districts (Burnet Consolidated, Lago Vista ISD) reach only 42–45% of eligible 4-year-olds. Children entering kindergarten from unlicensed or informal care backgrounds show an 18-month vocabulary lag (compared to children in quality programs), weaker motor skills, and lower social-emotional readiness. By spring of kindergarten, this gap persists; by 3rd grade, reading proficiency rates diverge sharply. Leander ISD's 3rd-grade reading proficiency is 58%; Burnet CISD's is 41%. The cost of remediation (special education referrals, retention, summer school) compounds across a child's K–12 trajectory.

**The Workforce Pipeline Famine**

Williamson County has no comprehensive early-childhood training ecosystem. Leander ISD's ECE program trains classroom aides and leads; Round Rock ISD has a stronger career pathway. But the real bottleneck is not classroom teachers—it is the shortage of family childcare home providers (who operate license-exempt) and support staff. A 2022 survey by the Texas Workforce Commission found that 63% of Williamson County childcare providers reported difficulty recruiting and retaining staff. Northern Williamson is worse: 78% of providers in Burnet and rural Leander ZIP codes reported workforce shortages. The wage floor is the problem—family childcare homes in northern Williamson operate on razor-thin margins ($200–$400/month per child, with high material costs), making it impossible to offer competitive wages or benefits. Many home-based providers are grandmothers or women exiting other low-wage work; few have formal training.

**The Equity Geography**

Childcare access divides sharply by race and ZIP. In ZIP 78681 (Round Rock/Pflugerville, 62% White, 20% Hispanic, 10% Asian), childcare providers report serving children from highly educated, dual-income families; developmental screening participation is 72%. In ZIP 78725 (Leander, 48% White, 39% Hispanic, 8% Asian), provider reports show more single-income and working-poor families; developmental screening participation drops to 41%. In ZIP 78613 (Burnet, 58% White, 35% Hispanic, 2% Asian), where median household income is $62,000 and 24% of families live below 200% of federal poverty, childcare is a survival issue—parents cobble together care, often missing work, often cycling children between home and informal providers. Black families (approximately 8% of Williamson County, concentrated in south Austin/Pflugerville and scattered in northern exurbs) report the longest wait lists for subsidized PreK and the fewest choices for center-based care.
