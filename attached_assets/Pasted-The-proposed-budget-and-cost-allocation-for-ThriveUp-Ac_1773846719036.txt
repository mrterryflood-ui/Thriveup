The proposed budget and cost allocation for ThriveUp’s WIOA Title I Youth program align tightly with federal guidelines, state context, and the real-world needs of youth ages 16–24 facing multiple barriers to employment. In today’s labor market, national data consistently show that young people—particularly those who are out of school, experiencing basic skills deficiencies, English learners, or facing system involvement—experience elevated unemployment and underemployment, limited access to credential-attainment pathways, and reduced lifetime earnings without targeted support. U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics data indicate that youth unemployment persists at higher rates than the general population, and recent WIOA performance reporting highlights a persistent gap in credential attainment and measurable skill gains for OSY (Office of Workforce Investment, USDOL, 2022–2023). Locally, our service area reflects this national pattern: approximately 18–22 percent of 16–24-year-olds are not engaged in work or school, with elevated concentrations of individuals who are housing-insecure, English learners, or in foster care or juvenile justice systems. These populations bear disproportionate burdens when access to career pathways is fragmented by competing costs—transportation, childcare, and digital divide—creating a compelling case for a holistic, asset-based program design that reduces friction across the pipeline from intake to post-exit outcomes. The budget narrative that follows demonstrates how ThriveUp’s ecosystemic approach—anchored in the MAP-GAP methodology, the Three Realities framework, and a 14-platform technology ecosystem—translates directly into compliant, cost-effective program delivery that maximizes opportunities for OSY, meets WIOA 14 required youth program elements, and tracks performance indicators with precision.

From a program design perspective, the proposed model rests on a robust theory of change that connects continuous gap analysis with targeted interventions and real-world work experiences. MAP-GAP—Monitoring, Assessing, Predicting with Gap analysis—serves as the backbone of the program’s improvement cycle: data are collected on participant barriers, progress toward credentials, and labor market relevancy; gaps are identified in real-time, and adaptive supports are deployed through a blend of platform-enabled services, mentoring, and labor market connections. The Three Realities—Research Reality, Political Reality, and Ground-Level Reality—inform every decision from employer engagement strategies to the sequencing of coursework and the timing of work-based experiences. This theory of change is operationalized through ThriveUp’s 14-platform ecosystem, each platform contributing a defined function that maps directly to the 14 required youth program elements: Tutoring and dropout prevention; Alternative secondary school services; Paid/unpaid work experience; Occupational skills training; Education offered concurrently with workforce preparation; Leadership development; Comprehensive guidance and counseling; Mentoring; Follow-up services; Financial literacy education; Entrepreneurial skills training; Labor market information; Transition to postsecondary activities; and Supportive services. The evidence base for this approach is strong: work-based learning and structured internships are associated with higher credential attainment and longer-term employment stability; mentoring, financial literacy, and concurrent education have demonstrated positive effects on persistence and earnings among OSY in multiple meta-analyses; and integrated case management tied to continuous performance monitoring consistently improves both engagement and outcomes (Rand Corporation reviews of youth workforce programs; MDRC syntheses of career pathways and WIOA implementation; TEGL guidance for WIOA program design). ThriveUp’s model translates these findings into a costable, scalable framework using the 14 platform components to ensure fidelity to evidence-based practice while allowing for local customization through data-driven decisions.

The target population and eligibility monitoring is designed to maximize OSY enrollment while maintaining alignment with WIOA’s 75 percent OSY funding requirement and the 20 percent minimum investment in work-based learning experiences. Our estimated program demographics anticipate that 75 percent of participants will be out-of-school youth, with a remaining 25 percent in-school youth who meet one or more barriers to employment (for example, basic skills deficiencies or English language learners) and who will benefit from the intense transition supports that precede or accompany postsecondary enrollment. Recruitment strategies will leverage local schools, juvenile justice partnerships, community-based organizations, faith-based networks, and LifeBridge benefits navigation channels to identify eligible youth who face barriers such as homelessness, foster care involvement, disability, or low-income status.
