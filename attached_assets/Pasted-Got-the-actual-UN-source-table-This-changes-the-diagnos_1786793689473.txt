**The US row, decomposed (2023 data, 2025 report)**

United States: HDI 0.938 (rank 17, tied with Liechtenstein and New Zealand) → IHDI 0.832. Overall loss 11.3%. Rank drop: −12 places. Coefficient of human inequality 10.7.

Component losses:

| Dimension | US loss | Iceland | Norway | Denmark | Japan | Canada | OECD avg |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Life expectancy | **5.5%** | 2.0 | 2.4 | 3.1 | 2.5 | 4.3 | 4.8 |
| Education | **2.7%** | 2.3 | 1.8 | 2.3 | 5.8 | 2.2 | 6.4 |
| **Income** | **23.9%** | 10.7 | 14.3 | 11.0 | 17.1 | 16.0 | 21.8 |

**Here is the finding.**

American *education* distribution is excellent — 2.7% loss, better than the OECD average of 6.4%, better than Japan (5.8%), Korea (6.2%), France (5.3%), and the whole very-high-HD group average of 5.0%. On educational attainment distribution, the US is genuinely near world-class.

The 11.3% penalty is driven almost entirely by **income**, with health as the secondary driver. US income inequality loss of 23.9% is more than double Iceland's 10.7% and Denmark's 11.0%, and above both the OECD average (21.8%) and the very-high-HD group average (20.1%).

**The income shares make it concrete:**

The poorest 40% of Americans hold 15.6% of income; the richest 10% hold 30.2%; the richest 1% hold 20.7%. Gini 41.3.

Against peers: Iceland's poorest 40% hold 23.9%, richest 1% hold 8.0%, Gini 26.1. Norway: 22.9 / 9.3 / 27.7. Denmark: 23.1 / 12.0 / 28.3.

The US top 1% captures **20.7%** of national income. That exceeds Colombia (17.9%) and is roughly level with Brazil (21.1%) and Mexico (21.6%) — countries whose overall inequality is far worse but whose *top-end concentration* the US now matches.

**And the health number is the one nobody expects.**

US inequality in life expectancy is 5.5% — higher than every single very-high-HD peer democracy in the table, and above the OECD average of 4.8%. It's not just that Americans die younger on average; **lifespan itself is distributed more unequally in the US than in any comparable rich democracy.** That's your earlier state-level finding — Louisiana vs. Massachusetts, Black vs. white maternal mortality — showing up in the UN's own aggregate.

**The rank drop is the DiD estimate in one number.** The US falls 12 places when inequality is accounted for. Among wealthy democracies, only Hong Kong (−16) and Singapore (−19) fall further. Czechia rises 14 places, Slovenia rises 9, Estonia 12, Slovakia 11 — poorer countries that convert less potential into more delivered.

**What this means for your platform:** don't build a general "equity index." The diagnosis is specific. Education distribution is not the American failure. **Income distribution and lifespan distribution are.** Your county-level instrument should weight those two dimensions heaviest, because that's where the recoverable 6 percentage points actually sit — and it means policy levers on income (wages, transfers, medical debt, coverage) and on health access are the high-yield interventions, not school-attainment programs alone.
