DIAGNOSTIC IMPLEMENTATION SCIENCE • WORKING DOCTRINE
DIAGNOSTIC IMPLEMENTATION SCIENCE
A Context-Adaptive Doctrine for Turning Intention Into Sustainable Real-World Performance
 
“Implementation is always local, even when strategy is global.”
 
Working doctrine and conceptual companion to aDIS / NSF innovation work
Purpose: establish a domain-agnostic but context-sensitive operating philosophy that can guide individuals, organizations, communities, programs, and software systems.
Core proposition: Diagnostic Implementation Science (DIS) is not a replacement for implementation science, program management, systems engineering, human-centered design, or domain expertise. It is a reasoning and operating architecture for selecting and integrating the right methods for the right problem, people, place, time, and conditions.
 
Status: conceptual doctrine for refinement, validation, operationalization, and empirical testing. It deliberately distinguishes proposed doctrine from established implementation-science literature.

1. Executive Doctrine
Traditional implementation science asks how evidence-based interventions are adopted, implemented, and maintained in real-world settings. That foundation remains valuable, but the implementation problem is not unique to healthcare. Organizations in education, logistics, public administration, defense, emergency management, business, community development, grants, contracting, and technology face the same recurring gap: an intended end state exists, yet actual performance diverges from it.
Diagnostic Implementation Science extends the operating logic without pretending that every discipline should use the same framework. Its first commitment is diagnosis before prescription. The system establishes current state, desired state, the gap between them, the conditions producing or sustaining that gap, the people affected, the evidence available, and the uncertainties that remain. Only then does it select tools.
The doctrine is simultaneously agnostic and adaptive. It is agnostic about preferred frameworks: CFIR and RE-AIM may be useful in one setting; Lean, Agile, PMBOK, systems engineering, human factors, logistics analysis, design thinking, qualitative inquiry, or statistical process control may be better elsewhere. It is adaptive because implementation changes as people, policy, technology, resources, environment, risk, and evidence change.
The doctrinal promise
• Know where you are before deciding how to move.
• Define where you are trying to go and for whom that end state matters.
• Diagnose the gap rather than treating symptoms as causes.
• Ask the next necessary question, not merely the questions on a checklist.
• Match the tool to the problem; never force the problem into the tool.
• Measure what informs decisions, not merely what is easy to count.
• Preserve essential purpose while adapting execution to legitimate context.
• Treat users, stakeholders, and affected communities as part of the implementation system.
• Build learning, maintenance, sustainment, escalation, and retirement into implementation from the beginning.

2. The 5W+H Multidimensional Reasoning Architecture
Who, What, When, Where, Why, and How are not six questions. They are six diagnostic dimensions. Each dimension can interrogate every other dimension, producing at least 36 first-order intersections before deeper recursive questions are considered. The objective is not to ask more questions for their own sake. It is to prevent premature closure and expose the relationships that determine whether implementation succeeds.
Dimension
Illustrative diagnostic scope
WHO
Affected, responsible, accountable, excluded, influential, capable, resistant, burdened, benefiting, deciding, maintaining.
WHAT
Current condition, desired condition, intervention, evidence, assumptions, dependencies, constraints, measures, thresholds, failure modes.
WHEN
Readiness, sequencing, cadence, trigger, intervention point, measurement point, escalation, scale, update, retraining, sustainment, retirement.
WHERE
Physical, organizational, cultural, socioeconomic, regulatory, digital, political, operational, supply-chain, jurisdictional context.
WHY
Purpose, mechanism, causal hypothesis, incentive, barrier, rationale, assumption, unintended consequence, priority, value.
HOW
Design, implementation, resourcing, governance, maintenance, security, monitoring, evaluation, communication, recovery, learning.
Cross-dimensional reasoning
A useful diagnostic question combines dimensions: What are we doing, for whom, where, when, why, and how? Why this particular What? How was the What selected? Who defined success? Where does the proposed How fail? How will we know When adaptation is warranted? Who has authority to make that adaptation, and Why? The intersections often contain more implementation intelligence than the isolated answers.
Second-order inquiry matters even more. For example: How do we know why an intervention worked for one population in one location but not for a similar population elsewhere? What changed between those contexts? Who experienced the difference? When did divergence begin? Which mechanism plausibly explains it? What evidence would falsify that explanation?

3. Context Is an Active Variable
The doctrine treats context as part of the causal and operational system, not as background description. A standardized strategy can encounter different implementation realities because populations, resources, rules, workforce, infrastructure, incentives, culture, geography, competition, climate, supply chains, and histories differ.
The Wilmington–Austin test
Consider a Walmart in Wilmington, North Carolina, and a Walmart in Austin, Texas. They may share a brand, enterprise systems, corporate standards, and strategic objectives. They are nevertheless different implementation environments. Customer mix, workforce, traffic patterns, regional suppliers, local regulation, weather exposure, housing costs, purchasing behavior, community relationships, and competitive markets can differ. Even two stores in the same city can diverge.
The correct implementation question is therefore not, “Did both stores follow the same plan?” It is: What must remain invariant and why? What may adapt? Adapt for whom? Where are the meaningful differences? When do they become operationally significant? How will those differences be detected? Who can authorize adaptation? What evidence shows that adaptation improved rather than degraded performance?
Adaptive fidelity
Diagnostic Implementation Science uses adaptive fidelity as a governing principle: protect core purpose, ethical boundaries, safety constraints, regulatory requirements, and critical outcomes while adapting implementation to context. Fidelity without contextual fit can produce rigid failure; adaptation without invariants can produce drift. The task is to govern the boundary between them.

4. Operating Cycle: From Sensing to Adaptation
The 5W+H matrix describes how the system interrogates reality. The operating cycle describes how it moves. The proposed cycle is: Sense → Question → Understand → Diagnose → Decide → Act → Measure → Learn → Adapt. It is intentionally non-linear. New evidence can return the system to diagnosis; implementation can expose a faulty assumption; measurement can reveal an unintended effect; a contextual change can invalidate yesterday’s decision.

The cycle should support explicit stop conditions, escalation thresholds, human-in-the-loop triggers, maintenance schedules, review cadences, and retirement criteria. “Sustainment” is not an afterthought; it is designed into the intervention.

5. Measurement: From Activity Counting to Decision Intelligence
Measurement exists to improve understanding and action. Many organizations over-measure activity and under-measure effect: meetings held, dollars spent, trainings completed, applications submitted, tasks closed. Those data may matter, but they do not establish whether the underlying condition changed.
DIS connects Conditions → Inputs → Decisions → Actions → Outputs → Outcomes → Effects → Learning, while retaining context around the chain. Each measure should answer a decision question: What is being measured? Why does it matter? For whom? Who owns the measure? Where is variation meaningful? When is it collected and reviewed? How reliable is it? What threshold requires action? What happens after the threshold is crossed?
Diagnostic measurement classes
• State measures: where the system is now.
• Gap measures: distance between current and desired state.
• Process measures: whether implementation is occurring as intended.
• Outcome measures: whether the target condition is changing.
• Context measures: environmental or organizational variables that alter interpretation.
• Equity/distribution measures: who benefits, who is burdened, and where effects differ.
• Experience measures: usability, trust, comprehension, burden, satisfaction, and lived experience.
• Risk and safety measures: thresholds, anomalies, failure modes, and escalation triggers.
• Sustainment measures: maintenance burden, capability retention, resource sufficiency, and durability.
• Learning measures: assumptions validated, hypotheses rejected, adaptations made, and residual gaps.
A metric that no longer informs a meaningful decision should be reconsidered. More data are not automatically better implementation.

6. Software Doctrine: The Interface Is Part of the Intervention
For software, implementation doctrine must connect backend logic, data architecture, models, UI, UX, user-centered design, accessibility, information architecture, workflow, engagement, and governance. A technically correct platform can still fail implementation if people cannot understand it, trust it, navigate it, or act through it.
Every screen, metric, notification, workflow, recommendation, and control should be traceable to purpose through the 5W+H matrix. What decision does this element support? Who needs it? Why is it shown? When should it appear? Where does it belong in the workflow? How should the user act on it? How will the system know whether it is helping or creating friction?
Design around the cognitive and operational journey
The interface should not mirror the database schema. It should mirror the user’s work. Complexity should be progressively disclosed. Different roles can require different views of a shared underlying truth because their authority, vocabulary, decisions, risk, and information needs differ.
Engagement as implementation evidence
Logins are weak evidence of implementation. Meaningful signals include task completion, decision quality, comprehension, time-to-action, abandoned workflows, repeated navigation, ignored alerts, support requests, manual workarounds, overrides, escalation patterns, and whether the platform reduces or increases cognitive burden. User behavior can reveal implementation defects.
Decision transparency
When a system scores, recommends, prioritizes, flags, or escalates, the user should see enough reasoning to exercise judgment. The UI should distinguish known facts, inferred relationships, uncertainty, missing information, and contested evidence. Transparency should be proportional to consequence and user responsibility.

7. Applied Example: Community Intelligence
A community platform built under this doctrine should not be a demographic dashboard. Its purpose is to build a living situation model that connects community conditions, assets, lived experience, service availability, policy, resources, geography, institutional behavior, stakeholder networks, disparities, and outcomes.
What it should help answer
• What is happening, to whom, where, and since when?
• Why might the condition exist, and what competing explanations remain?
• Which assets, services, policies, institutions, and informal networks already influence the condition?
• Who is missing from the data or decision process?
• Where are service deserts, duplication, bottlenecks, or inequitable burdens?
• What action is feasible now, for whom, by whom, and with what resources?
• How will the community know whether conditions improved rather than merely whether services were delivered?
• When should a response scale, adapt, escalate, or stop?
The interface should permit residents, community organizations, funders, researchers, public agencies, and service providers to see a coherent evidence base while receiving role-appropriate views. Community voice is not decorative qualitative data; it is implementation evidence that can explain why administrative indicators look the way they do.

8. Applied Example: Grants and Funding Intelligence
A DIS-oriented grants platform should not stop at opportunity discovery, eligibility, or proposal generation. It should diagnose the relationship among organizational identity, mission, capabilities, past performance, strategic priorities, community need, funder behavior, opportunity timing, competition, partnerships, implementation capacity, compliance burden, and post-award performance.
The decision is not simply “Can we apply?”
The platform should help answer: Why this opportunity? Why this organization? Why now? Funding for what and for whom? What evidence demonstrates need? Where will implementation occur? Who must partner? What capabilities are missing? What is the probability-adjusted value of pursuit? What is the opportunity cost? How will the award be executed and sustained? What reporting and performance obligations follow? When should leadership deliberately decline an opportunity?
This reframes grant seeking from search-and-write activity into implementation intelligence. The platform becomes capable of linking pre-award diagnosis to post-award execution and learning. The proposal is no longer the end product; it is one artifact in a larger implementation lifecycle.
UI implication
The user should see the next best decision, the evidence supporting it, unresolved gaps, ownership, deadlines, readiness, dependencies, and the path from opportunity to performance. A 92% “match” without explaining readiness, risk, missing capabilities, or execution burden is not sufficient decision support.

9. Applied Example: Government Contracting
A contracting platform operating under the same doctrine should connect mission need, customer priorities, acquisition pathways, historical awards, budget signals, incumbent performance, competitors, teaming requirements, organizational capabilities, certifications, regulatory constraints, readiness, proposal burden, delivery risk, and recompete dynamics.
The system should distinguish an attractive solicitation from an executable opportunity. It should ask not only what the government is buying, but why the requirement exists, who owns the mission problem, where the requirement operates, when acquisition decisions occur, how the customer historically buys, what evidence demonstrates capability, what teaming closes gaps, and how contract performance will be measured.
From opportunity matching to mission alignment
A useful interface would show mission fit, capability fit, evidence strength, customer knowledge, acquisition timing, competitive position, teaming gaps, compliance risk, bid/no-bid rationale, and execution readiness. After award, the same diagnostic architecture should continue into mobilization, delivery, performance surveillance, learning, and recompete. This closes the artificial separation between business development and implementation.

10. Beyond Platforms: A Simpler Operating Model for Organizations
Diagnostic Implementation Science is intended to simplify disciplined management, not add another layer of bureaucracy. Program management, project management, quality improvement, operations research, strategy, and evaluation remain valuable. The doctrine determines when and why to use them.
A business operating rhythm
Cadence
Diagnostic purpose
Continuous
Sense material changes, risks, user/customer signals, exceptions, and dependencies.
Weekly
Identify deviations, blockers, ownership, near-term decisions, and required adaptations.
Monthly
Examine trends, context, resource sufficiency, outcomes, and cross-functional effects.
Quarterly
Reassess assumptions, strategy-to-execution alignment, portfolio priorities, and capability gaps.
Event-triggered
Escalate when thresholds, safety conditions, uncertainty, opportunity, or external changes warrant.
Lifecycle
Plan maintenance, sustainment, scaling, modernization, transition, and retirement.
Meetings and reports should exist only when they serve a decision or coordination need. The organization should be able to trace major work to an intended outcome, owner, rationale, evidence base, context, dependencies, measures, review point, and adaptation rule.
Human judgment and automation
Automation should perform work that can be reliably standardized, monitored, or calculated. Human judgment should be required where consequence, ambiguity, ethics, novelty, accountability, or uncertainty exceed defined bounds. The doctrine asks not only when a human enters the loop, but why, who, with what authority, using what information, and how the decision is documented and reassessed.

11. Relationship to aDIS and the NSF Innovation Thesis
This doctrine can complement aDIS by supplying a broader implementation philosophy around an adaptive diagnostic system. aDIS can be framed as a technical instantiation of selected doctrinal principles: continuous sensing, multidimensional diagnosis, contextual adaptation, bounded decision-making, verification, escalation, measurement, learning, and governed adaptation.
The distinction should remain clear. Diagnostic Implementation Science is the proposed general doctrine. aDIS is a technical architecture/system that can operationalize portions of that doctrine. An NSF research program can then test specific mechanisms rather than attempting to prove the entire philosophy at once.
Potential researchable propositions
• Multidimensional diagnostic questioning improves identification of implementation-relevant gaps compared with static checklist approaches.
• Context-aware adaptation can improve fit without violating prespecified invariants.
• Explicit separation of facts, assumptions, hypotheses, unknowns, and disagreements improves decision traceability.
• UI/UX designed as part of the intervention improves comprehension, meaningful engagement, and appropriate action.
• Closed-loop sensing, measurement, learning, and adaptation can reduce implementation drift and shorten time-to-correction.
• Governed human-in-the-loop thresholds can improve accountability in high-consequence or uncertain decisions.
These are hypotheses to operationalize and test, not established claims. That distinction strengthens scientific credibility and gives aDIS a disciplined path from doctrine to measurable technical objectives.

12. Evidence Base, Boundaries, and Research Agenda
The established implementation-science literature supports several components of this doctrine while not, by itself, validating the proposed cross-domain synthesis. CDC/NIOSH describes implementation science as the study of methods to increase adoption, implementation, and maintenance of evidence-based interventions in real-world settings and notes multidisciplinary roots extending beyond healthcare. Recent work also supports deliberate adaptation, user-centered design, human factors, and attention to heterogeneous local workflows.
A 2024 Annual Review of Public Health article emphasizes the need to distinguish adaptive implementation strategies from adaptive study designs and recommends specifying core functions. Recent technology-implementation research integrates user-centered design, human factors, and implementation science to improve fit across heterogeneous settings. A 2024 Implementation Science study used iterative user-centered design to improve the usability of a dissemination-and-sustainability planning platform. These findings support the doctrine’s insistence that context, adaptation, usability, and implementation cannot be treated as separate concerns.
What remains novel and must be tested
• Whether the 5W+H multidimensional matrix is a reliable diagnostic architecture across non-health domains.
• How to determine the optimal depth of recursive questioning without producing analysis paralysis.
• How to select domain tools dynamically and transparently.
• How to encode adaptive fidelity as machine-enforceable invariants plus context-sensitive execution.
• How to quantify diagnostic quality, decision quality, learning velocity, implementation drift, and time-to-correction.
• How UI/UX signals can safely contribute to implementation diagnosis without overinterpreting user behavior.
• How the doctrine performs across organizations, populations, geographies, and levels of complexity.

13. Doctrine: Non-Negotiable Principles
1. Diagnose before prescribing. Do not select an intervention before establishing current state, desired state, gap, context, evidence, and uncertainty.
2. Implementation is always local, even when strategy is global. Common objectives do not erase meaningful contextual differences.
3. 5W+H is a reasoning matrix, not a checklist. Every dimension can interrogate every other dimension.
4. Ask the next necessary question. Recursive inquiry continues until evidence is sufficient for a proportionate decision.
5. Match the tool to the problem. Frameworks serve implementation; implementation does not serve frameworks.
6. Separate knowledge from belief. Facts, assumptions, hypotheses, unknowns, and disagreements must remain distinguishable.
7. Measure for decisions. Metrics must connect to understanding, action, accountability, or learning.
8. Preserve adaptive fidelity. Protect core purpose and invariants while adapting legitimate execution variables.
9. The interface is part of the intervention. UI, UX, UCD, accessibility, workflow, and engagement are implementation mechanisms.
10. Design sustainment at the beginning. Maintenance, resourcing, governance, scaling, transition, and retirement are lifecycle requirements.
11. Govern automation and human judgment. Escalation must reflect consequence, ambiguity, ethics, novelty, uncertainty, and accountability.
12. Learning changes the system. Evidence that does not alter understanding or action when warranted is not a functioning learning loop.
North-star question
Given who and what are involved, where and when the work occurs, why the outcome matters, and how the system currently behaves: what is the next right action, what evidence justifies it, how will we know what happened, and what will we change when reality teaches us something new?
