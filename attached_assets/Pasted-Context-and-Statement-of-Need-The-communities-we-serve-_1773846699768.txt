Context and Statement of Need

The communities we serve face persistent, multi-layered barriers to youth employment and economic mobility. Nationally, youth unemployment remains markedly higher for young people with barriers—such as limited educational attainment, housing instability, foster care involvement, English language learner status, disability, involvement with the juvenile or adult criminal system, and economic insecurity—than for their more advantaged peers. In 2023, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that the unemployment rate for youths aged 16–24 was significantly elevated for those outside traditional education-to-work pipelines, with pronounced disparities by race/ethnicity, gender, and locality. The gap between opportunity and access for In-School Youth (ISY) and Out-of-School Youth (OSY) is not merely cyclical; it is structural, reflecting gaps in access to high-quality career pathways, robust paid/unpaid work experiences, and extended, person-centered supportive services that address barriers in education, housing, health, and safety. The data are compelling: OSY are six to eight times more likely to be unemployed than their peers who remain in or complete secondary or postsecondary pathways; they experience elevated risk for housing instability, involvement with the courts, health disparities, and low credential attainment. In many communities, including the districts we serve, recent estimates suggest that as many as 30–40% of OSY face at least three concurrent barriers—poverty, limited English proficiency, and basic-skills deficiencies—creating a compounded risk of long-term labor market exclusion if not effectively addressed through coordinated workforce development strategies.

Locally, our region has faced persistent barriers to youth attainment and employment that are not adequately addressed by traditional one-off training programs. High school graduation rates lag behind state averages in several districts served by the Local Workforce Development Board (LWDB), while drop-out and disconnection from postsecondary paths remain elevated among populations of color and youth with disabilities. Employer surveys conducted in the past two years show a demand for entry- to mid-level occupational skills across health care, information technology, skilled trades, and advanced manufacturing. Yet, many local employers report that prospective hires lack workplace readiness competencies, soft skills, and explicit credentials tied to the in-demand pathways that align to career ladders with measurable wage growth. Simultaneously, health inequities and housing insecurity influence attendance, concentration, and consistent participation in training, internship, and mentoring activities. The confluence of these factors creates a critical opportunity for a comprehensive, youth-focused intervention that crosses education, workforce preparation, and employment with an intentional focus on equity, health, safety, and long-term economic advancement.

In this grant proposal, ThriveUp — in concert with The Collaborative Advocate (TCA) and Minority Center of Excellence (MCE) — positions itself to catalyze a transformative, evidence-based approach to youth workforce development grounded in MAP-GAP, SALP, and the Three Realities framework. Our approach recognizes that successful pathways require continuous gap analysis, fidelity to best practices, and a rigorous attention to the political, research, and ground realities shaping workforce development in our communities. We propose a 14-platform ecosystem that translates knowledge from research and policy into scalable, practice-based, youth-centered interventions designed to produce durable outcomes. We will deploy a structured continuum of services that aligns with the 14 required youth program elements of WIOA Title I, with explicit attention to the six primary performance indicators that the U.S. Department of Labor uses to evaluate success and accountability. In our plan, at least 75% of funds will serve OSY, and at least 20% of funds will be invested in Work Experience (WEX), including paid/unpaid internships, pre-apprenticeships, and on-the-job training (OJT). We anticipate delivering impact through a federated, technology-enabled delivery system that harmonizes education, workforce preparation, and labor market connections across multiple platforms, while maintaining rigorous evaluation and fidelity monitoring through RPLICE and a SALP-aligned fidelity framework.

The problem is not simply the absence of training or internships. It is the misalignment of education with the realities of the labor market, the fragmentation of services that youth and families must navigate, and the insufficient emphasis on personal health, safety, and resilience as foundations for stable employment. Our program narrative is designed to address these realities by leveraging a robust theory of change that links a youth-centered intake and case management process with evidence-based career pathways, intensively mentored internships, applied skill-building, and sustained post-exit support. It is a plan that responds to a pressing community need for durable, credential-bearing, market-relevant pathways that advance youth toward economic self-sufficiency, opportunity for advancement, and lifelong learning. The design is anchored in the Three Realities framework—Research Reality, Political Reality, and Ground-Level Reality—so that our activities remain grounded in up-to-date evidence, informed by policy directions and funding opportunities, and responsive to the lived experiences and voices of the youth and families we serve.

In addition to traditional elements of WIOA Title I Youth programming, ThriveUp’s approach integrates our unique differentiators: MAP-GAP as a continuous improvement framework that emphasizes monitoring, assessing, predicting gaps, and implementing targeted interventions; SALP to certify fidelity and alignment with program design across all platforms; and the Three Realities concept to balance cutting-edge research with pragmatic political feasibility and direct, lived experiences on the ground. The 14-platform ecosystem we deploy is not a collection of disconnected services but an integrated, multi-layered system designed to deliver a coherent and comprehensive experience for youth: ThriveUp provides the core educational and career-prep foundation; The Incubator ensures the design of scalable, evidence-based programs; MCE brings minority-focused business and entrepreneurship components; LifeBridge activates community engagement and navigation support; RPLICE supervises fidelity and program integrity; Sankofa Health anchors health equity and preventive care; M2C Transition focuses on military-to-civilian transitions; SafeReport supports safety and anonymous reporting; Perfectly Different addresses neurodiversity and disability; WholeMind Learning emphasizes SEL and mental health; PillScheduler supports medication adherence and health management; SafeCogniCare addresses elder care; Better Science Lab advances research and evaluation; and ISSS strengthens school safety. Together, these platforms create a holistic ecosystem that leverages cross-platform collaboration and data sharing to maximize outcomes while respecting youth autonomy and dignity.

In describing our vision, it is essential to note that the proposed program aligns with the comprehensive work of the LWDB and current federal guidance on youth programs, including the requirement to deliver 14 program elements, to prioritize OSY, and to sustain employment and credential attainment after exit. Evidence-based practice supports the use of integrated service-delivery models that combine career exploration, academic enrichment, and work-based learning with holistic supports such as mentoring, financial literacy, guidance and counseling, and transition services. Key research in the field demonstrates that youth programs combining high-fidelity mentoring with authentic work experiences and team-based problem solving yield higher credential attainment and wage gains than programs that focus solely on training or placement. Our approach explicitly anchors these elements within a robust theory of change that uses data-driven decision making and continuous quality improvement to adapt to changing labor market conditions and youth needs. The design is further informed by evidence on the value of employer engagement and work-based learning in improving placement rates and earning potential, particularly when combined with targeted skills training aligned to in-demand occupations and sector-based pathways. By weaving together the 14 program elements, the platform ecosystem, and the MAP-GAP fidelity framework, we create a durable mechanism to transform youth trajectories at scale, with a strong emphasis on equity and inclusion.

Program Design and Theory of Change

Our program design rests upon a theory of change that links intermediate processes to long-term outcomes, with explicit attention to the causal pathways by which we expect to move OSY and ISY toward employment, credential attainment, and sustained labor-market success. At its core, the theory recognizes that youth outcomes are driven by four interdependent domains: education and credential attainment, work readiness and skills development, labor market connections and employment opportunities, and supportive services that address barriers to participation. The theory is operationalized through three interlocking levers: (1) the MAP-GAP continuous improvement cycle, (2) the Six-Stage SALP fidelity protocol, and (3) the Three Realities framework, which anchors the design in evidence, policy alignment, and ground-level relevance.

MAP-GAP provides a four-phase life cycle for program design and delivery. The Monitoring phase establishes ongoing data collection and performance surveillance through a unified data system that captures metrics across all program elements and platforms. The Assessing phase uses a standardized gap-analysis toolkit to identify individual and group-level barriers, readiness for training, and alignment to occupational pathways. The Predicting phase applies predictive analytics to forecast outcomes at the client level and to anticipate resource needs, enabling proactive interventions. The Gap-Closing phase translates assessment results into concrete, targeted interventions such as customized tutoring plans, individualized career coaching, and structured work experiences. The entire cycle feeds back into management decisions, enabling continuous improvement, fidelity checks, and course corrections before problems escalate. This loop ensures that the program is responsive to youth needs, labor market shifts, and the evolving policy environment.

The Six-Stage fidelity protocol under SALP ensures that platform deployments maintain high fidelity to program design, while allowing for context-sensitive adaptation. The stages cover program initiation, onboarding and training of staff and partners, alignment of all platforms to the shared logic model, ongoing monitoring of service delivery with real-time dashboards, periodic fidelity reviews (including site visits and data audits), and formal re-design sessions when fidelity drifts are detected. The SALP framework uses measurable fidelity indicators such as the percentage of youth who complete all applicable elements within a given quarter, the alignment rate of work experiences to the target occupation family, the timeliness of follow-up services, and youth satisfaction scores. This rigorous fidelity regime reduces the risk that highly integrated, platform-based approaches devolve into fragmented services and ensures consistent implementation across partner sites and platforms.

Three Realities anchors the design to three crucial lenses: the Research Reality connects our work to the best available evidence from longitudinal studies, meta-analyses, and program evaluations that show what works for youth employment and credential attainment; the Political Reality keeps us aligned with federal, state, and local policy levers—funding cycles, wage thresholds, apprenticeship standards, and the evolving priorities of the LWDB—to ensure sustainability and strategic alignment; and the Ground-Level Reality centers youth voices, family experiences, and community contexts, using participatory advisory structures, youth councils, and community listening sessions to co-create program activities and refining methods. Together, these realities help ensure that our interventions are not only effective in a controlled research context but also feasible, acceptable, and scalable in real-world settings.

The MG-PATR framework (Monitoring, Guiding, Predicting, Assessing, Translating, and Realigning) informs the continuous improvement architecture across all platforms. It blends operational obedience to fidelity with adaptive capacity, enabling us to respond to daily operations and strategic shifts quickly. MG-PATR emphasizes robust data governance, transparent performance reporting, and timely decision-making, coupled with a culture of learning and accountability. It demands explicit documentation of decisions, rationale for course corrections, and measurement of impact at the scale required by WIOA Title I Youth programs. In practice, MG-PATR translates together the fidelity checks of SALP with the data-informed adjustments produced by MAP-GAP, ensuring that improvements in service delivery translate into measurable outcomes such as higher credential attainment, better employment rates, and stronger earnings gains.

To ensure a coherent, scalable, and integrated approach, ThriveUp leverages a 14-platform ecosystem that forms the backbone of service delivery. Each platform contributes specific competencies and resources, while cross-platform collaboration ensures a seamless client experience. ThriveUp is the hub for education and career readiness, offering tutoring, alternative schooling options, and comprehensive counseling. The Incubator supports program design, development, and customization, helping to translate research into practical curricula, instructional strategies, and performance measures. MCE brings an explicit focus on minority business leadership and entrepreneurship, enabling youth to explore independent pathways and small-business ownership as viable career options, including micro-enterprise development and business planning. LifeBridge serves as the community voice and benefits navigation platform, helping families access housing, healthcare, transportation, and social supports that remove participation barriers. RPLICE implements fidelity monitoring and data quality assurance across all platforms, ensuring data integrity and consistent application of the program logic. Sankofa Health anchors health equity, providing preventive care, health literacy, trauma-informed care, and access to youth-friendly health services. M2C Transition focuses on military-to-civilian transitions for eligible youth with military links or service backgrounds, including credentialing and job placement in relevant civilian sectors. SafeReport provides safe, anonymous reporting channels to address safety concerns, bullying, harassment, or abuse in educational or workplace settings, with a process for timely follow-up. Perfectly Different concentrates on neurodiversity and disability inclusion, offering accessible instruction, accommodations, and targeted supports to ensure all youth can complete training and apprenticeship pathways. WholeMind Learning centers SEL and mental health, delivering evidence-based practices such as mindfulness, peer mentoring, and social-emotional learning curricula. PillScheduler integrates health management supports—medication adherence, appointment reminders, and chronic disease management—to sustain participation in training, health, and work-based activities. SafeCogniCare extends supports to elder care contexts and family caregiving realities, recognizing intergenerational labor market participation. Better Science Lab provides the research and evaluation backbone—designing rigorous studies, collecting data, and translating findings into program improvements. ISSS (Integrated School Safety Services) strengthens school and program safety, building environments that minimize risk and maximize youth engagement. Together, these platforms constitute a robust ecosystem designed to deliver comprehensive services, reduce fragmentation, and enable youth to progress along well-defined career pathways with credible credentials.

Our theory of change is designed to deliver durable outcomes through deliberate sequencing and alignment of activities across the platform ecosystem. In the initial intake and eligibility phase, youth are identified through strategic partnerships with high schools, community organizations, juvenile justice agencies, shelters, and primary care providers. We use standardized screening tools to identify barriers to education and employment, including learning gaps, housing insecurity, health needs, English-language proficiency, disability status, and prior involvement with the justice system. Youth then receive a comprehensive assessment to identify career interests, wage expectations, and potential barrier-removal strategies. The MAP-GAP framework drives subsequent steps: creating individualized learning plans that integrate academic supports, career exploration, and industry-recognized credentials aligned to in-demand occupations; placing youth into high-quality work experiences that are relevant to their chosen pathways; aligning health, safety, mental health, and social supports to ensure sustained participation and well-being; and ensuring continuous feedback loops that allow timely adjustments to goals, pacing, and supports.

The program design anchors the career pathways in in-demand sectors within the regional labor market and national priorities, including health care, information technology, advanced manufacturing, logistics and supply chain, energy and environmental sustainability, and entrepreneurship. Each pathway is reinforced by the 14 elements required under WIOA Title I Youth programs: Tutoring and study skills/dropout prevention; Alternative secondary school services; Paid/unpaid work experience; Occupational skills training; Education offered concurrently with workforce preparation; Leadership development; Supportive services; Adult mentoring for at least 12 months; Follow-up services for 12 months post-exit; Comprehensive guidance and counseling; Financial literacy education; Entrepreneurial skills training; Services that provide labor market information; and Transition to postsecondary activities. These elements will be delivered through the integrated platform architecture and cross-platform collaborations, ensuring that youth experience a coherent, multi-faceted journey rather than disconnected interventions.

Our approach is deeply evidence-based, drawing on findings from meta-analyses and large-scale program evaluations that underscore the value of integrated, long-term mentoring, early work-based learning experiences, credential-rich pathways, and sustained post-exit supports. The integration of paid and unpaid work experiences with structured training has consistently shown improved credential attainment, higher earnings, and increased job stability when combined with targeted case management and academic supports. The incorporation of mentorship, financial literacy, and youth leadership development is associated with stronger engagement and lower dropout rates, particularly for youth facing multiple barriers. We will also rely on employer-driven design principles, ensuring that the work experiences and credentials align with current industry needs and wage growth potential, a finding supported by sectoral labor market analysis and employer surveys. The Three Realities framework ensures that the program remains adaptable to shifting policy directions and resource allocations while preserving fidelity to best practices. Finally, the RPLICE mechanism ensures data integrity and continuous quality improvement across all platforms, so that decisions about service delivery, staffing, and investments are justified by reliable data.

Target Population and Eligibility

Our target population includes youth aged 16–24 who reside in the LWDB catchment area and who face one or more barriers to education, training, and employment. The OSY share will constitute no less than 75% of participants. The eligibility criteria align with WIOA requirements: they must be a youth who is not enrolled in any school or training program or who is enrolled but not in a full-time program and who meets the age eligibility, with a focus on those who face at least one barrier to employment or education. The barriers include, but are not limited to, the following: school dropout or risk of dropping out; basic skills deficiency; being an English learner; involvement with the juvenile or criminal justice system; homelessness or foster care involvement; pregnancy or parenting; disability; economic disadvantage or income below the threshold; a lack of occupationally relevant credentials; and limited access to transportation, child care, or housing; or health barriers such as chronic illnesses or mental health concerns that impede consistent participation. Our recruitment strategy hinges on a multi-channel approach that leverages school outreach, juvenile justice partnerships, homeless/housing service providers, pediatric and school-based health centers, community-based organizations, faith-based groups, libraries, and social media campaigns to identify and engage eligible youth. We will codify a referral protocol through MOU pathways with the LWDB and partner organizations, ensuring that referrals are timely, consent-based, and privacy-protected.

The demographic composition of the population will reflect the region’s diversity, with explicit attention to equity goals for historically underserved groups, including but not limited to Black and Latino/a/x youth, Indigenous youth, immigrant and refugee youth, youth with disabilities, and youth in foster care or housing instability. We anticipate serving a substantial share of youth from rural and urban settings, each presenting distinct barriers and opportunities. We will implement targeted recruitment strategies to reach English learners through bilingual staff and translated materials, and we will ensure accessibility for youth with disabilities through accommodations in communication formats, sign language interpretation, accessible facilities, and assistive technology. Our recruitment plan includes a two-pronged approach: (1) proactive outreach with community partner events, school-based presentations, and provider-led referrals, and (2) open access outreach through digital campaigns and community networks. The program will also incorporate a youth advisory board and community participation mechanisms so that the program reflects the needs and priorities of the youth it serves.

We will screen potential participants using standardized, evidence-based screening instruments that assess barriers and baseline assets in education, health, housing, transportation, safety, and financial literacy. A key feature of the intake process is a person-centered, trauma-informed approach that respects youth voice and autonomy, ensuring informed consent and assent as appropriate. The intake process will capture demographic data, barrier indicators, educational attainment, prior work experiences, and expressed career interests, as well as health and safety concerns that could affect participation. Based on the assessment results, youth will be assigned to a career pathway that aligns with their interests and the region’s in-demand occupations. We will ensure that 75% of participants are OSY by purposefully allocating outreach and enrollment efforts toward OSY and aligning recruitment messages with OSY-specific barriers and opportunities. Additionally, when appropriate, we will identify ISY participants who require support to re-engage with education or training and connect them with alternative schooling or transitional services to support their long-term educational goals.

The program’s target outcomes are aligned with WIOA performance indicators and the needs of the community. Specifically, we aim to enroll an annual cohort of approximately 600 youth, with OSY comprising at least 450 participants (75%), ISY comprising the remaining 150 participants (25%). We anticipate serving a substantial fraction of youth with barriers such as housing instability (at least 30%), disability (15–20%), English learners (25–35%), previously incarcerated youth (5–10%), pregnant or parenting youth (5–10%), and foster care alumni (10–15%). Our recruitment and outreach strategies will also identify and enroll youth who have multiple co-occurring barriers to ensure the OSY-specific focus of our program is realized. By design, the program will include supports to address non-academic barriers that often impede training access and progress, including transportation assistance, childcare subsidies, digital access and devices, and stipends for participation in work experiences.

Service Delivery Model

The service delivery model is a cohesive, phased journey that begins with screening, moves through integrated training and work experiences, and culminates in credential attainment and post-exit supports. The intake-to-exit pathway is designed to ensure that every participant experiences a well-structured sequence of services, including the 14 required program elements, delivered through the platform ecosystem while maintaining fidelity through SALP and MAP-GAP processes.

Intake and eligibility determination represent the initial stage. Youth are identified through partner referrals, school-based outreach, community events, and health-care and social-service gateways. A dedicated program navigator—assigned to each cohort and cross-platform—facilitates the intake process, coordinates eligibility determinations, and introduces the participant to their personalized career pathway. The navigator forms the core of the participant’s early experience, building trust, setting expectations, and orienting youth to the 14 program elements and the 14-platform architecture. Admission decisions consider the youth’s barriers, interests, and readiness, ensuring that OSY participation meets the mandatory 75% threshold and that the program can deliver the required resources and supports to meet each participant’s needs.

Following intake, a comprehensive assessment is conducted to identify educational gaps, skill deficiencies, health and safety needs, and career interests. The MAP-GAP tool is deployed to structure the gap analysis, identify the most impactful interventions, and establish a baseline for measuring progress. The assessment data feed into the Individualized Employment Plan (IEP), which outlines a sequential path toward credential attainment and employment. The IEP specifies the sequence of services each youth will receive, including academic supports, career exploration activities, skill-building exercises, and work experiences. The IEP also identifies the primary platform responsibilities and the cross-platform coordination required to deliver integrated services. The IEP is dynamic and subject to revision as youth progress and as labor-market conditions shift, with safeguards to ensure that youth remain on track to complete the required elements.

A central feature of the service model is deliberate sequencing of activities to align with the in-demand pathways and to maximize outcomes across all six primary performance indicators. The six pathways include health care and social assistance, information technology, advanced manufacturing and logistics, entrepreneurship and small business development, green energy and sustainability, and construction trades. For each pathway, we identify the core competencies and credentials that correspond to industry-recognized standards, such as industry-recognized certifications, apprenticeship participation, and postsecondary certificates or degrees. The platforms work in concert to deliver the required elements: Tutoring and study skills/dropped-out prevention, alternative secondary school services, and other academic supports are delivered primarily through ThriveUp and WholeMind Learning; Paid/unpaid work experiences and OJT are implemented via the LifeBridge, MCE, and SafeCogniCare platforms, with direct supervision from the employer engagement lead and the work experience coordinator; Occupational skills training is delivered through a combination of The Incubator and partner institutions; Leadership development is integrated via the Perfectly Different and LifeBridge platforms; Supportive services, financial literacy education, and follow-up services are delivered through the parallel tracks provided by LifeBridge, Sankofa Health, and ISSS, with a cross-platform emphasis on mentoring through the Adult Mentoring element; Comprehensive guidance and counseling will be provided by ThriveUp’s counseling staff and the RPLICE-based fidelity team; Entrepreneurial skills training will be anchored in MCE and supported by the incubator/entrepreneurship components; Labor market information will be disseminated through platforms with strong employer engagement, including SafeReport and the employer liaison team; Transitions to postsecondary activities will be supported through the Incubator and the MCE ecosystem to connect youth with college or apprenticeship opportunities and to facilitate dual enrollment where applicable.

Work experiences are a central pillar of the model. We will allocate at least 20% of program funds to work experiences, consistent with WIOA requirements. Work experiences will include paid internships with local employers in targeted industries, unpaid internships with partner organizations, pre-apprenticeships within registered apprenticeship programs, and a mix of on-the-job training opportunities within the private, non-profit, and public sectors. We will tailor the work experiences to each youth’s IEP, ensuring alignment with the chosen career pathway, with capacity to escalate to longer-term employment or apprenticeship if youth demonstrate readiness and employer interest. Supervisors, mentors, and program staff will be trained in youth development principles and trauma-informed practices to ensure a safe and supportive experience. Employers will receive support through the employer engagement team, including wage subsidies, professional development resources, and access to a skilled pool of motivated youth with strong career potential.

Education offered concurrently with workforce preparation is integrated into the program from the outset. Youth are connected with tutoring, credit recovery, and alternative schooling as needed, with alignment toward credential attainment in the target pathway. For ISY who require an alternative path to graduation, we will provide comprehensive options that may include high school equivalency programming, dual enrollment, or online coursework within the ThriveUp system. Education modules are designed to be modular and stackable, allowing participants to earn micro-credentials along the path toward a credential or degree. Where necessary, cross-agency partnerships with local school districts and alternative schools will ensure continuity of academic progress with the workplace learning experiences.

Leadership development occurs concurrently with technical skill-building to cultivate self-efficacy, problem-solving, teamwork, and civic engagement. This includes participation in student leadership opportunities, youth advisory councils, project-based learning experiences, peer mentoring roles, and community service activities aligned to pathway goals. The leadership development component is delivered through the Perfectly Different platform, which uses neurodiversity-aware practices to ensure inclusive leadership cultivation across participants with diverse learning profiles. We will also embed opportunities for youth to practice presentation and professional communication in real-world contexts, such as employer engagement meetings, exhibit demonstrations, and capstone projects that showcase skill application and problem-solving.

Supportive services address a broad set of barriers that commonly impede program participation and success. We will provide transportation assistance (bus passes, gas cards, or mileage reimbursements), childcare subsidies, stipends during work experiences, health and wellness resources, and targeted academic supports to address learning gaps. These supportive services will be delivered through LifeBridge and ISSS channels, with coordination through the navigator to ensure timely access. Financial literacy education is integrated into monthly workshops and one-on-one coaching, including budgeting, banking, credit-building, debt management, and understanding earnings progression. We will also provide information on benefit programs and entitlements where appropriate, ensuring youth and families have access to the broader social supports they need to participate fully in the program.

Adult mentoring is a central element, with at least 12 months of mentoring available to each participant. Mentors are trained in youth development, trauma-informed practice, motivational interviewing, and career coaching. Mentors facilitate goal setting, problem-solving, and resilience building, supporting youth through challenges, including the transition from training to employment. We will monitor mentoring engagement to ensure consistency and quality, maintaining a ratio of no more than a defined maximum number of participants per mentor to sustain meaningful relationships. Follow-up services continue for 12 months post-exit, with a structured set of check-ins designed to support job retention, credential maintenance, and ongoing education or training. This post-exit support includes ongoing mentoring, alumni networks, referrals to additional training or employment opportunities, and connection to community resources that support lasting success.

Comprehensive guidance and counseling are delivered by a dedicated team of career navigators and counselors, who use standardized counseling protocols, standardized career assessments, and goal-tracking tools to help youth navigate academic and career decisions. The counseling includes individual and group sessions, as well as crisis support and referrals to mental health and health-care services as needed. Financial literacy, debt management, and budgeting are integrated into counseling sessions, with content aligned to the needs of youth in the program. The inclusion of entrepreneurial skills training, including business planning and bootstrapping, addresses the aspiration for self-employment that is particularly relevant in a region with a dynamic small-business ecosystem.

Entrepreneurial skills training is provided through MCE’s entrepreneurship ecosystem and The Incubator. Youth interested in entrepreneurship will gain knowledge in market research, business planning, financing, marketing, and operations. They will have access to mentorship from minority business owners and opportunities to pilot micro-enterprises or to develop prototypes for products or services with potential for scale. This pathway will create opportunities for youth to pursue startup ventures as a legitimate career pathway that complements more traditional employment routes, particularly in sectors where self-employment and micro-enterprises are part of the regional economy. Labor market information is embedded across all activities, with data on in-demand occupations, wage ranges, and job growth rates provided through platforms such as ISSS and RPLICE. Participants will have access to labor market data in simple, usable formats and will be guided by career coaches and mentors to interpret, apply, and translate these data into action.

Transition to postsecondary activities is supported through a combination of postsecondary navigation and college-readiness activities, and, where feasible, dual enrollment opportunities with local community colleges or workforce training providers. Our strategy focuses on education-to-work and work-to-credential transitions, ensuring that youth are not simply placed into jobs but are prepared to pursue further credentialing and advancement.

For the 14 program elements, we will implement robust sequencing plans and tracking across all four phases of service delivery: enrollment, service initiation, progress toward credentials, and exit. Each element has a defined set of activities, expected outputs, and outcomes, with clear alignment to the platform architecture. The Tutoring and Study Skills/Dropout Prevention element is delivered through ThriveUp and WholeMind Learning, with specialized supports for literacy, numeracy, and college-readiness strategies. Alternative Secondary School Services are delivered through ThriveUp, the IS-level partnerships with local school districts, and approved alternative schooling arrangements for ISY participants. Paid/Unpaid Work Experience is delivered through LifeBridge, Sankofa Health, SafeReport, and M2C Transition, ensuring that youth have meaningful, supervised opportunities to practice skills in real workplaces. Occupational Skills Training is delivered through The Incubator, with content aligned to recognized credentials and pathways. Education offered concurrently with workforce preparation is integrated across platforms, ensuring that youth simultaneously develop academic and job-ready skills. Leadership development is offered through Perfectly Different, LifeBridge, and youth leadership councils. Supportive services and Follow-Up services are delivered through LifeBridge and ISSS, with the guidance of mentors and case managers. Adult mentoring is delivered through a combination of staff and community mentors, with a minimum 12-month relationship. Comprehensive guidance and counseling is provided by ThriveUp and partner specialists. Financial literacy education is delivered through LifeBridge and Sankofa Health channels. Entrepreneurial skills training is delivered through MCE and The Incubator. Labor market information is shared through RPLICE and the employer engagement team. Transition to postsecondary activities is supported through the Incubator and MCE platforms.

The target performance outcomes are aligned with the six primary performance indicators required by WIOA Title I Youth programs. We anticipate achieving an employment rate (Q2 after exit) of at least 68 percent and an employment rate (Q4 after exit) of at least 72 percent across the participant cohort. We target a median earnings of at least $26,000 in Q2 after exit, with credential attainment within four quarters for at least 60 percent of participants who exit with a credential. We expect to achieve a measurable skills gain for at least 55 percent of participants who engage in training activities that yield gains. And we aim to demonstrate substantial employer engagement, defined as at least 60 percent of participants who experience a work-based learning opportunity that leads to employment or a firm job offer within 12 months of exit. We will track these metrics using the unified data system, with real-time dashboards for program staff, supervisors, and the LWDB.

We will also monitor intermediate targets in each career pathway, such as the proportion of youth completing credential-bearing modules, the share of youth securing paid internships, the proportion of youth achieving certifications aligned with in-demand occupations, and the rate of job placement within the first three months after exit. We anticipate that OSY will achieve stronger gains on these metrics due to the concentrated emphasis on wage trajectories, credential attainment, and targeted work experiences.

The program will ensure compliance with all the 14 program elements through a structured mechanism of platform-aligned activity gateways and milestone-based progress assessments. Each participant will be assigned to a pathway with defined milestones, including academic milestones, work-experience milestones, credential milestones, and transition milestones. We will implement a platform-based progress-tracking tool that records progress against milestones, flags at-risk participants, and triggers appropriate interventions. We will also implement a youth-friendly, web-based portal that enables youth and parents or guardians to monitor progress, access resources, and communicate with staff. The portal will integrate with the RPLICE data platform to ensure fidelity monitoring and data integrity across all platforms and activities.

Implementation Timeline

The implementation timeline is organized into phased rollout stages with clear milestones
