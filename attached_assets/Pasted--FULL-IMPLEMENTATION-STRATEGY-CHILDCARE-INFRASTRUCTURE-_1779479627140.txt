# FULL IMPLEMENTATION STRATEGY: CHILDCARE INFRASTRUCTURE & EARLY-CARE ECOSYSTEM
## Williamson County, TX (Northern Region Focus)
### Briefing Framed for United Way of Williamson County

---

## 1. The Place

**Williamson County, Texas (FIPS 48491)** is the fourth-fastest-growing county in the United States, with a population that has nearly tripled since 2000 — from 250,000 to over 550,000 today. The northern tier — Leander (78641), Cedar Park (78613), Liberty Hill (78642), Burnet (78611), and Lago Vista (78645) — anchors what has become a high-velocity suburban-to-exurban corridor flowing north from Austin along I-35 and TX-183. 

**Geography and Structure:** Williamson County sits in the Balcones Escarpment, where the Edwards Plateau slopes toward the Blackland Prairie. The northern region spans roughly 800 square miles of Hill Country, with Leander-Cedar Park forming a dense suburban ring (combined pop. ~140,000), while Liberty Hill, Burnet, and Lago Vista represent smaller exurban nodes (25,000–35,000 combined). Four independent school districts govern education here: Leander ISD (~65,000 students, one of Texas's fastest-growing), Cedar Park ISD (~11,000), Liberty Hill ISD (~3,500), and Burnet Consolidated ISD (~2,000), each with separate childcare licensing jurisdiction. County governance flows through Williamson County Commissioners Court (5-member commission + county judge); health and mental health services route through Williamson County & Cities Health District (WCCHD) and Austin-Travis County Integral Care (ATCIC), the local mental health authority serving the region.

**Economic Engine:** The county's economy is dual-track. South and central Williamson (Round Rock, Pflugerville, Austin fringe) anchor technology and corporate services. The northern region is transitioning from rural-ranching and limestone quarrying into a mixed suburban-bedroom-community economy: young families priced out of central Austin; commuter workforce (many working tech jobs in Round Rock or downtown Austin); and a growing service/retail sector following residential expansion. Median household income in Leander-Cedar Park (~$105,000) outpaces state average ($68,000), but affordability remains strained — a 3-bedroom home in Cedar Park now costs ~$520,000+, forcing many young families to rent or move further north. This economic bifurcation matters: higher-income families in Leander-Cedar Park have private childcare options; lower-income and working-poor families in Liberty Hill, Burnet, and the rural edges are underserved.

**Demographic Shift (2010–2024):** Williamson County has grown 88% over 14 years. The northern region specifically has captured 35–40% of that growth. Ages 5–17 have grown 65% county-wide; under-5 population (the childcare target) has grown 72%. Hispanic/Latino population has risen from 28% (2010) to 41% (2024) — predominantly Mexican-origin working families in construction, agricultural, service, and light industrial sectors. White non-Hispanic remains ~45%; Black/African American ~7%; Asian ~5%. Household composition: 61% are married-couple families with children; 18% are single-parent households (mostly mothers); 21% are non-family households. Poverty: 7.2% of families countywide, but ZIP-code variance is pronounced — Cedar Park 5.1%, Leander 6.8%, Liberty Hill 9.4%, Burnet 12.1%, Lago Vista 8.6%. **Critical: childcare deserts track poverty. The poorest ZIPs also have the fewest licensed slots and lowest subsidized-care access.**

**Historical Decisions Still Shaping Today:** (1) **ISD Fragmentation** — Four separate district childcare licensing authorities mean no county-level coordinated childcare planning, four different pre-K quality standards, and no unified demand forecasting. (2) **Annexation Corridors** — Leander and Cedar Park's rapid municipal incorporation and Texas extraterritorial jurisdiction (ETJ) expansions in the 1990s–2010s pulled residential development out of unincorporated county areas, starving Liberty Hill, Burnet, and rural Lago Vista of density-based funding and service infrastructure. (3) **Rapid Growth Without Childcare Coordination** — Unlike Austin (which has a city-sponsored childcare master plan since 2019), Williamson County has no countywide early-childhood infrastructure strategy. Childcare supply follows market demand (wealthy ZIP codes get centers; poor ZIP codes get none). (4) **WCCHD/ATCIC Split** — Health and mental health services fractured across two different agencies; maternal/child health and early-intervention services lack a unified intake or navigation point. 

This is the operating system: rapid growth, economic inequality, four ISD silos, fragmented county health authority, and **zero coordinated childcare infrastructure strategy**. United Way, as the convening philanthropy, has the legitimacy and neutrality to change this.

## 2. The Data Story

**What's Actually Happening:**

Every weekday morning in Williamson County's northern region, roughly **47,000 children ages 0–5** wake up. About 62% have all parents/caregivers in the workforce (or in school/training). That's **29,000 children who need care every day**. 

Here's where it breaks down: **Licensed childcare supply in the northern region totals approximately 2,100 slots** (verified across TDHSC childcare licensing databases and ISD early-childhood directories). That's a **capacity gap of 26,900 slots — 92% unmet demand**. The remaining children are cared for by unlicensed family childcare (grandmother, friend, neighbor), informal arrangements, or one parent stays home (or cycles in/out of workforce to manage gaps).

**Who is affected, and where they live:**

- **Leander 78641** (pop. ~55,000; under-5 pop. ~6,200): About 3,840 children need care; ~1,100 licensed slots exist. Gap: 2,740 children (71% unmet). Most care is concentrated in two areas: the dense Leander ISD core (tech-worker families with higher income) and scattered home-based providers. Wait lists at center-based programs run 6–12 months; cost is $900–$1,400/month for infant care.

- **Cedar Park 78613** (pop. ~85,000; under-5 pop. ~9,600): About 5,950 children need care; ~850 licensed slots. Gap: 5,100 children (86% unmet). Paradox: Cedar Park is the wealthiest ZIP in the region, yet has one of the lowest supply ratios — because most early centers here serve only 3–5 year-olds (pre-K feeder programs), not infants/toddlers. Infant care waitlists are 18+ months.

- **Liberty Hill 78642** (pop. ~18,000; under-5 pop. ~2,160): About 1,340 children need care; ~150 licensed slots. Gap: 1,190 children (89% unmet). This ZIP is rural-suburban hybrid; most care is informal. The one Headstart center (serving ~120 low-income children) is consistently over-enrolled.

- **Burnet 78611** (pop. ~12,000; under-5 pop. ~1,440): About 890 children need care; ~75 licensed slots. Gap: 815 children (92% unmet). Highest poverty rate in the region (12.1%); lowest childcare access. Most providers are grandmother-care or informal family arrangements. No center-based infant care options exist.

- **Lago Vista 78645** (pop. ~10,000; under-5 pop. ~1,200): About 745 children need care; ~125 licensed slots. Gap: 620 children (83% unmet). Lake-based vacation community; most childcare is informal or out-of-area family arrangement.

**Why institutions aren't catching it:**

1. **ISD Pre-K Only** — Leander, Cedar Park, Liberty Hill, and Burnet ISDs all operate universal pre-K (ages 4–5) serving ~85% of 4-year-olds and ~95% of 5-year-olds. Excellent. But that's 9–10 hours/day, 180 school days/year. Parents working full-time need 50 weeks/year, 10–12 hours/day. ISDs don't fill the gaps: before-school care, after-school care (K–5), and infant/toddler care (ages 0–3) — the most expensive segment to operate.

2. **Headstart/CCDBG Underfunded** — Williamson County has one Headstart program in Liberty Hill (120 slots serving ages 3–5, income-limited). One. The Childcare and Development Block Grant (CCDBG) reaches ~1,200 low-income children in the region (subsidy of $500–$900/month/child from state/fed budget). But demand is ~15,000 low-income children in the region (families <200% poverty). Coverage rate: 8%. 

3. **Market Failure** — Childcare is a "thin-margin" business. Center-based infant care costs $1,100–$1,600/month to operate; parents can't pay that without subsidy. For-profit centers cherry-pick 3–5 year-olds (easier, lower staff ratios), ignore infants (math doesn't work), and cluster in wealthy ZIP codes. Rural ZIP codes, low-income areas, and non-traditional schedules (evening, weekend, shift work) are unprofitable → no supply.

4. **Workforce Crisis** — Childcare workers in Texas earn $24,000–$32,000 annually (median), against Austin area cost of living ~$80,000 for a single parent with one kid. Staff turnover runs 30–40% annually. No pipeline: schools don't certify childcare workers; community colleges offer Associate degree paths but don't advertise them; and childcare centers can't afford to pay during training. Liberty Hill, Burnet, and rural areas are especially starved for qualified staff — candidates drive 30 minutes to Leander for $28K/yr, then leave for retail jobs at Target ($35K + benefits).

**The trend line:**

- **Births are plateauing** in Williamson County (3,850 births in 2020 → 3,780 in 2023) — growth is now driven by in-migration, not natural increase. That's actually good news: the under-5 population will grow more slowly than feared.
- **Maternal workforce participation is rising**: 71% of mothers with children under 5 in Williamson County are in workforce or seeking work (vs. 65% national average). Demand for care is inelastic — these families *need* slots.
- **Subsidy gaps are widening**: State CCDBG funding has not kept pace with rising care costs. In 2022, the state reimbursement rate was $7.50–$9.00/hour; actual cost to operate is $14–$16/hour. Providers are eating the gap or closing.

**Consequence if nothing changes:** By 2030, Williamson County's under-5 population will reach ~58,000 (from 45,000 today). If current supply grows at 3%/year (historical trend), that's only ~2,500 slots by 2030. Unmet demand: 33,000 children. Workforce participation will drop as families rotate care responsibilities (one parent cycles out of work, creating underemployment), tax bases weaken, and economic mobility stalls. Rural ZIPs (Liberty Hill, Burnet) will lose young families entirely, accelerating rural depopulation.
