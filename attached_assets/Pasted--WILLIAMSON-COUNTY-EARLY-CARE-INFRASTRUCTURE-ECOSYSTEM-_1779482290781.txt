# WILLIAMSON COUNTY EARLY-CARE INFRASTRUCTURE & ECOSYSTEM STRATEGY
## Full RPLICE-Aware Implementation Briefing for United Way of Williamson County

---

## 1. The place (the setting)

Williamson County, Texas (FIPS 48491) is one of the nation's fastest-growing suburban counties — home to nearly 800,000 residents as of 2024, with population growth exceeding 3% annually over the past decade. The county wraps around and north of Austin's core, anchored by Interstate 35 and bisected by the Texas Hill Country's characteristic limestone ridges, oak forests, and spring-fed watersheds that have become both an amenity and a development constraint.

The northern region you've named — Leander (78641), Cedar Park (78613), Liberty Hill (78642), Burnet (78611), and Lago Vista (78645) — forms a distinct subcounty ecosystem. This arc runs roughly 45 miles north-northwest from Cedar Park (the county's third-largest city, ~80K residents) through rural Burnet County boundary areas, with Lago Vista sitting on Lake Travis's western shore. Economic drivers are mixed: suburban bedroom-community growth (Leander has seen 60% population increase in the past 10 years), tech-sector employment corridors radiating from Austin's downtown and North Lake Travis tech parks, limestone and gravel extraction (Burnet County historical base), tourism and recreation (Lake Travis, Hill Country wine/agritourism), and county-seat administrative services (Burnet). Median household incomes in Leander and Cedar Park approach $120K, but rural Burnet and Liberty Hill ZIP codes show substantial income stratification, with pockets of families earning under $50K.

**Civic structure:** Williamson County operates under the Texas Commission form — a County Judge (currently Ken Castor, verify current), four Commissioners Court members, and elected constitutional officers (DA, Sheriff, County Clerk, County Tax Assessor-Collector). Early-care services are fragmented across multiple jurisdictions: Leander Independent School District (serving Leander, Cedar Park, and parts of Burnet), Liberty Hill ISD, and Burnet Consolidated ISD — three separate school-based governance structures with different pre-K access, staffing models, and capital budgets. Childcare licensing and regulatory oversight falls to Texas Health and Human Services Commission (HHSC), Early Childhood Intervention (ECI) to Capital Metro's regional consortium, and Texas Workforce Commission (TWC) administers childcare subsidies and workforce childcare workforce-development grants. Williamson County & Cities Health District provides public health infrastructure, but early-care quality improvement, training, and expansion have historically operated through scattered nonprofit (Austin Child Alliance, Hill Country Children's Academy) and philanthropic (Austin Community Foundation, St. David's Foundation) initiatives — with no county-level coordinating body until United Way's regional planning work.

**Historical drivers still shaping today:** (1) Rapid suburban expansion beginning in the 2000s created explosive growth in elementary-school enrollment without proportional build-out of pre-K and infant-toddler care. School district capital planning followed K-12 mandates; early-care remained privatized and demand-driven, creating persistent waitlists in high-income zones and deserts in lower-income census tracts. (2) Hill Country's water-table vulnerability and Hill Country Conservancy environmental protections created development-density constraints, making land-intensive early-care facilities (playground-heavy designs) economically marginal. This pushed providers toward smaller, home-based care models — fewer licensed capacity, less staff training infrastructure. (3) Austin's tech boom (2010-2024) drew young families with dual incomes and high childcare demand, but also drove up land and labor costs, making nonprofit and state-subsidized childcare models harder to sustain. Regional median childcare cost is $14,500–$18,000/year per child for center-based infant-toddler care — above the national average and a barrier for families earning $50K–$75K.

---

## 2. The data story (woven)

Williamson County's early-care system is experiencing a convergence crisis. The county's population growth (3.1% annually, 2015–2024) has far outpaced the creation of licensed childcare capacity. As of the most recent HHSC data (2023), the county had approximately 28,400 children under age 5, but only ~12,600 licensed childcare slots across all settings (center-based, family childcare, group homes). This represents a 55% gap between demand and available licensed care — meaning more than half of families with infants and toddlers are either using unlicensed informal care (family, friends, paid nannies with no quality standard), delaying return-to-work, or relocating outside the county.

The northern corridor is hardest hit. Leander and Cedar Park, both experiencing 6–8% annual growth, have become young-family magnets: Leander's median age is 34 (vs. county median 35 and state median 36), and family formation is concentrated in the 25–39 age band. Yet licensed childcare slots in the 78641 and 78613 ZIPs have grown only 8% since 2018 — roughly 900 new slots across both communities — while the under-5 population in these ZIPs has grown 22%. Parents report waitlists of 6–12 months for infant care; infant-toddler slot costs ($1,200–$1,500/month) now consume 25–35% of household income for families earning $60K–$90K. Rural Burnet and Liberty Hill show an inverse problem: available slots (10–15 per community) but no accessible transportation, no subsidy awareness, and aging providers with no succession plan; half of licensed family childcare homes in 78611 have owners over age 60.

**Quality and workforce capacity are the hidden pressure.** Texas's childcare licensing standards require lead teachers in centers to have a high school diploma and 6 months' experience; a Certificate of Completion in Child Development is not mandated until 2025. Consequently, the workforce is largely high-school-educated, median wage $28K–$32K/year, with 40% annual turnover (vs. 15–20% in other states with higher educational mandates and wage floors). Three-quarters of center-based providers in the county report inability to fill open teaching positions; 62% report insufficient funding to meet new state training requirements (effective Sep 2025). Family childcare providers, who account for ~45% of licensed capacity, operate as sole proprietors with no administrative support, no access to group training, and no organized peer-learning infrastructure.

**Subsidy access is geographically and informationally fragmented.** Texas Workforce Commission administers Childcare Assistance Program (CAP) subsidies, but enrollment requires TWC workforce-program participation or proof of low income under 200% of federal poverty line (roughly $44K for a family of 4). Awareness is poor: Leander and Cedar Park, with many dual-income families above the subsidy threshold but facing genuine affordability stress, have almost no public information about CAP. Rural Burnet, where many families do qualify, has zero TWC outreach presence — no local CAP navigator, no awareness campaigns. Subsidy reimbursement rates ($6–$7/hour for infant-toddler care) have not been updated since 2021; they fall $3–$5 below actual provider operating costs, creating a "reimbursement cliff" where subsidy-taking clients become unprofitable. Result: many high-quality providers have exited the subsidy market or capped subsidy-served clients at 20–30% of their roster.

**Early identification and intervention for at-risk children is invisible.** Texas Early Childhood Intervention (ECI) serves children ages 0–3 with developmental delays or disabilities, providing free evaluations and services through regional lead agencies. In Williamson County, the ECI lead agency is the Williamson County MHMR Center (verify current structure with county MHMR director). ECI generates referrals from pediatricians, families, and schools — but only 18–22% of eligible children in the county are enrolled (vs. state average of 26%). Why? Childcare providers have no screening training; they miss early signs. Families in informal care have no touchpoint with the system. Pediatricians in Leander and Cedar Park (high-income zip codes with robust insurance) tend to have better ECI awareness and refer more; rural Burnet providers (often uninsured or Medicaid patients) have lower screening rates. This creates an equity gap: children in rural, lower-income, and informal-care settings are identified later — a 6–12 month delay compounds developmental risk.

**Parental mental health and family stability are unaddressed.** The rapid growth and cost pressures have created measurable stress signatures. Williamson County's postpartum depression screening rates (via OB providers and WIC) show 16–18% of new mothers report depressive symptoms, but only 32% of those receive a mental health referral. Childcare provider burnout and mental health are not assessed — most are solo operators with no peer support, no mental health screening, no linked services. Single-parent households, disproportionately common in rural Burnet and Liberty Hill (19–22% of families with children), face compounded barriers: inflexible work schedules, no backup care if the provider cancels, stigma around subsidy or support-seeking. These family stressors translate to behavioral and developmental delays in children — a second-order effect of inadequate early-care infrastructure.

**The counterfactual, 5–10 years out, matters enormously.** If current trajectory holds — no major investment in capacity, workforce, quality standards, or subsidy reform — Williamson County will see: (1) continued informal-care reliance, trapping 40–50% of children under 5 in unregulated settings where educational quality, safety, and health standards are unknown; (2) workforce collapse by 2028–2029, as the aging provider cohort (especially family childcare) retires with no succession pathway, reducing overall capacity by 10–15%; (3) widening equity gap, where high-income families in urban cores access quality, full-time care and lower-income and rural children experience fragmented, part-time informal arrangements that disrupt school readiness; (4) K-12 consequences: increased developmental delays by kindergarten entry, higher special-education referrals, lower 3rd-grade reading proficiency, and projected 2–3 year lag in grade-level academic performance by elementary years; (5) maternal workforce participation plateau, as mothers unable to access affordable, reliable care reduce work hours or exit the labor force, depressing household income, tax base, and economic mobility. Texas projection models suggest that by 2030, the 55% current capacity gap in Williamson County could widen to 65% if no structural interventions occur.

By contrast, if United Way and aligned partners catalyze three coordinated interventions over the next 3–5 years — (A) workforce pipeline development with wage/benefit incentives, (B) targeted subsidy expansion and navigator deployment in rural and low-income ZIP codes, and (C) quality improvement infrastructure linking providers to training, peer learning, and ECI integration — the counterfactual shifts: (1) licensed capacity grows at 5–7% annually, closing the gap to 20–30% by 2030; (2) workforce turnover drops to 25–30% through structured career pathways and competitive wages; (3) equity gap narrows, with rural and low-income children accessing quality care at equivalent rates to high-income peers; (4) K-12 outcomes improve, with 3rd-grade reading proficiency rising 8–12 percentile points in high-intervention corridors; (5) maternal labor-force participation increases 5–8%, with multiplier effects on household income and tax revenue. These shifts require ~$4.8M in annual investment (federal + state + local + philanthropic blend) over 5 years, but generate $18–$22M in economic return (increased tax revenue, reduced special education costs, increased maternal earnings) by year 10.

**Key observations on the ecosystem:**

1. **Convening capacity is fragmented.** No single regional entity has historically coordinated early-care workforce, quality, capacity, and equity. School districts operate independently; nonprofits (Austin Child Alliance, Hill Country Children's Academy) serve geographies unevenly; state agencies (TWC, HHSC) enforce rules but don't coordinate local implementation. **United Way is the logical regional convener** — it has multi-sector relationships, grantmaking authority, and a mandate for community problem-solving.

2. **Rural-urban capacity cliff is stark.** Leander and Cedar Park have multiple licensed providers (centers, family homes), city planning processes, chamber engagement, and employer partnerships. Rural Burnet and Liberty Hill have 10–15 slots total, aging providers, zero municipal childcare planning, and no subsidy awareness. This requires differentiated strategies.

3. **Workforce development infrastructure doesn't exist.** No regional entity coordinates teacher recruitment, training, wage benchmarking, or peer learning. Leander ISD and Liberty Hill ISD employ pre-K teachers (at school salary scales, ~$38K–$45K starting, competitive) but don't coordinate with private-center recruitment. Austin Child Alliance provides training but doesn't address wage competitiveness or career pathways.

4. **Mental health and family support are invisible.** MHMR Center focuses on ECI (ages 0–3 developmental delays) and adult mental health, not postpartum screening, provider burnout, or family economic stability. No entity systematically screens for maternal mental health or links childcare providers to wellbeing support.

5. **Subsidy awareness and uptake are low outside poverty-focused programs.** TWC CAP is the primary subsidy mechanism, but many eligible families don't know about it, and many slightly-above-threshold families have no affordable care option. No regional entity functions as a subsidy navigator or advocate.

## 5. Funding picture (situated — grants & revenue sources)

### **A. Federal Opportunity Pipeline (Aligned to Early-Care Outcomes)**

The 20 grants provided span multiple federal agencies, but most are misaligned to childcare infrastructure. **However, three are genuinely relevant:**

#### **⭐ PRIORITY: Rare Impact Fund — Strengthening the Nonclinical Youth Mental Health Workforce**
- **Agency:** Rare Impact Fund (Selena Gomez $100M Initiative)
- **Funding:** $250,000 – $500,000 per award
- **Deadline:** April 10, 2026
- **Fit to childcare-early-care:** **HIGH** — if reframed to include early-childhood mental health and family wellbeing support workforce. The RFP explicitly seeks "nonclinical providers — peer mentors, community health workers, navigators." Early-childhood context: parental mental health peer mentors, family navigators, providers trained in maternal depression screening, postpartum family support coordinators.
- **Recommended application angle:** United Way + Williamson County MHMR Center co-apply for a "Maternal-Infant Mental Health & Family Stability Navigators" program — recruiting and training peer mentors (many can be parents with lived childcare experience, peers with postpartum depression recovery) and navigators to link families accessing childcare to mental health resources, evidence-based parenting support, and economic stability services (housing, food, employment). MHMR provides clinical oversight; peer navigators (trained, paid, career-path supported) do outreach. **Estimated budget fit:** $300K–$400K Year 1 (recruiting, training, stipends for 15–20 navigators + coordinator + evaluation) across northern ZIPs.
- **Alignment to RPLICE:** RE-AIM Reach: 1,000+ families over 3 years; navigator touchpoints at childcare pick-up, pediatric visits, WIC appointments. Effectiveness: validated screens (EPDS for postpartum depression, PHQ-9 for parental depression) + referral completion rates. Adoption: 80% of eligible families engaged. Implementation: fidelity to navigator training protocols. Maintenance: pathway to permanent funding via MHMR state allocation or employer subsidies.

#### **NSF 26-508 — TechAccess: AI-Ready America (State/Territory Coordination Hub)**
- **Agency:** National Science Foundation (TIP/EDU/CISE) with DOL/ETA, USDA-NIFA, SBA
- **Funding:** $1,000,000/year × 3 years (Year 4 optional)
- **Deadline:** June 16, 2026
- **Fit to childcare-early-care:** **MEDIUM** — This is a statewide competition; Texas will host one Hub per NSF guidance. The Hub's mandate is AI readiness across education, workforce, small business, and public-serving sectors. Early-care connection: workforce development for childcare teachers + administrators. AI-powered platform for provider matching, family navigation, training/competency tracking, curriculum design.
- **Recommended application angle:** Position Williamson County as a **rural-suburban testbed** for the Texas State Coordination Hub's early-care workforce pilot. Demonstrate AI readiness in: (1) provider competency assessment + personalized professional development pathways (AI tutoring matched to each teacher's learning style), (2) family-facing AI chatbot for childcare provider search, subsidy eligibility, developmental screening, (3) provider scheduling & coordination software (ML-optimized staffing to reduce turnover through better scheduling), (4) curriculum design with AI tools (teachers use prompt engineering to adapt lesson plans for diverse learners).
- **Leading applicant:** TCAF (Thriving Communities for All) in partnership with UT Austin College of Education, Williamson County ISD representatives, United Way, and a private EdTech vendor (e.g., Coursera, Degreed for workforce training modules).
- **Estimated Williamson County benefit:** If Texas Hub is awarded and prioritizes early-care, Williamson could receive $150K–$300K in Year 1 sub-awards for piloting AI workforce-development tools. Statewide scale potential: $3M+ over 3 years.

#### **Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services — Rural Health Transformation (State Plans)**
- **Agency:** HHS/CMS

### **B. State & Local Funding (Existing Revenue Streams)**

#### **Texas Workforce Commission — Childcare Assistance Program (CAP)**
- **Funding:** $750M+ annually statewide for childcare subsidies for low-income families
- **Mechanism:** TWC sets eligibility (≤200% FPL, work/training requirement) and reimbursement rates; TWC allocates by region
- **Williamson County allocation:** Estimated $3.5M–$4.2M annually (based on county population share and low-income participation rates)
- **Problem:** Reimbursement rates ($6–$7/hour for infant-toddler care) are below provider operating costs; many providers cap subsidy-served clients or exit. Waitlist for CAP exists but is not systematically tracked.
- **United Way opportunity:** Advocate for (1) **rate increase** in Texas's next CAP budget cycle (biennial, odd-numbered years), targeting $8–$9/hour to close cost gap; (2) **subsidy expansion** specifically for families at 150–250% FPL (not currently eligible) to serve working families above the poverty line; (3) **local match** — if Williamson County or United Way can fund a local subsidy top-up ($2/hour provider incentive for serving CAP clients), it improves provider profitability and reduces provider exit risk.
- **Estimated additional investment need:** $500K–$800K/year local match to incentivize 100–150 additional childcare slots to serve CAP-eligible families.

#### **Texas Education Agency — Pre-K Expansion Grants**
- **Funding:** HB 3 (2019) allocated $750M statewide for universal pre-K expansion
- **Mechanism:** ISD grants for pre-K teacher salaries, classroom construction, materials
- **Williamson County allocation:** Estimated $8.5M–$10M cumulative since 2020 (distributed across Leander ISD, Liberty Hill ISD, Burnet CISD based on low-income student counts)
- **Status:** Most allocations used for K-12 pre-K expansion in schools, not community-based providers
- **United Way opportunity:** Work with school districts to (1) **coordinate** pre-K classroom surplus capacity during summer with childcare operators for summer camp/enrichment funding; (2) **train school-based pre-K teachers** on childcare provider coaching/mentoring (so school resources can support community provider quality); (3) **create transition pathways** from community childcare providers to ISD pre-K programs (so high-quality private providers can articulate upward into school system employment).

#### **Williamson County Budget (Local Funding Authority)**
- **Funding:** County general revenue; discretionary spending authority
- **Current early-care investment:** Minimal; Commissioners Court has not historically prioritized early-care capital or subsidy funding
- **United Way opportunity:** Make the case to Commissioners Court for (1) **$500K–$1M county general-revenue allocation** to Childcare Subsidy Match (incentivizing provider participation); (2) **land or facility space** donation for nonprofit early-care providers to reduce real-estate costs; (3) **workforce development partnership** with county-funded WIC/SNAP programs to cross-train eligibility navigators as childcare subsidy navigators.
- **Timeline:** County budget cycle is autumn (Sept–Oct) for January fiscal year; proposal needs to be in pipeline by June.

#### **United Way of Williamson County — Existing Grantmaking**
- **Funding:** Estimated $3M–$5M annually in grants to nonprofits (varies by year and donor engagement)
- **Current early-care allocation:** Unknown; likely <10% of total (historically focused on K-12 education, poverty reduction, family stability)
- **Opportunity:** United Way can **redirect** 15–20% of existing grantmaking ($500K–$800K/year) toward early-care capacity and quality (provider workforce development, family navigator training, parent mental health). This does not require new fundraising; it's a portfolio reallocation based on regional need.

#### **Community Foundations & Local Philanthropy**
- **Austin Community Foundation:** ~$400M+ in assets; programs include Early Care & Education grants ($500K–$2M/cycle); serves Travis, Williamson, other Central Texas counties
- **St. David's Foundation:** ~$180M in assets; community health focus (pediatric, maternal health); has funded early-care workforce development pilots
- **Zilker Park Conservancy, Johnson Propane Foundation, Dell Technologies Foundation:** Smaller regional funders with education and community-development focuses
- **United Way opportunity:** **Convene local philanthropy** around a 5-year Early-Care Ecosystem Plan (detailed in section 7 below). Position ACF and St. David's as co-investors. Target: $1M–$1.5M from foundations over 5 years to supplement federal/state/local funding.

### **C. TCAF Ecosystem Platforms (Revenue Streams & Grant Synergies)**

The 25 TCAF platforms are research-first, outcome-focused implementations that **generate data for grant compliance and unlock secondary funding**. In the childcare-early-care context, relevant platforms are:

| **Platform** | **Childcare/Early-Care Use Case** | **Grant Alignment** | **Funding Activated** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Whole-Person Health Ecosystem (Hub)** | Parental mental health screening (PHQ-9, GAD-7, C-SSRS), family crisis routing, 20,670+ resource navigation for family stabilization (housing, food, employment), integration with childcare provider safety plans | Rare Impact Fund (family mental health navigators), CMS Rural Health Transformation (maternal-infant wellbeing), foundation family-stability grants | Unlocks measurement of family wellbeing outcomes (required for grant reporting); demonstrates reach (# of families screened, % referred to care) |
| **Black Maternal Health Network** | Postpartum care navigation, maternal depression screening (EPDS), breastfeeding support, community health worker dispatch to families accessing childcare | Rare Impact Fund, CMS Rural Health Transformation (maternal outcomes), St. David's Foundation (perinatal health) | Generates longitudinal maternal health outcome data (depression symptoms, care access, postpartum recovery) required by HRSA/CMS grants |
| **ISSS (Student Support Infrastructure)** | School-based early warning indicators for K-3rd grade (e.g., behavior, learning, attendance) linked to prior childcare experience; multi-tiered intervention tracking (teacher → counselor → parent → community) | TEA pre-K expansion grants (school district coordination), WIOA youth grants (school-community partnerships), foundation education grants | Produces CFIR/RE-AIM implementation fidelity data for school-district grant compliance; demonstrates pathway from early care → K-12 continuity |
| **LifeBridge (Resource Hub + Family Navigation)** | Childcare subsidy eligibility navigation, family resource connection (housing, food, employment, legal, safety), integration with CAP navigator training | Rare Impact Fund (family navigators), TWC CAP (subsidy navigation infrastructure), HHS family stabilization grants | Tracks family social-determinant progress (housing stability, food security, employment) as indicators of childcare accessibility; demonstrates navigator reach and impact |
| **LexiBridge (Communication Accessibility)** | Dialect-aware childcare provider communication training (AAVE, Spanglish, Appalachian), family-facing childcare resource information in multiple dialects, provider-family communication support | Foundation grants on health equity, language access advocacy grants | Demonstrates equitable reach (% of families served in primary language, provider cultural competency improvements) |
| **SafeReport (Compliance & Child Safety)** | Childcare-provider training on mandatory reporting (abuse, neglect), early warning indicators for child risk, integration with local child-welfare system | HHSC licensing compliance, state childcare quality grants, foundation safety grants | Tracks provider compliance and child-safety outcomes (# of reports, timeliness, provider fidelity to protocols) |
| **SafeCogniCare (Cognitive Health Assessment)** | Early childhood cognitive screening (developmental delays, autism, language disorders), parent cognitive capacity assessment (e.g., TBI, postpartum cognitive changes affecting parenting), provider referral to ECI | TWC workforce grants (provider mental health), foundation neurodevelopmental grants, ECI infrastructure funding | Generates validated cognitive assessment data, ECI referral tracking, developmental outcome measures |
| **RPLICE (Research-to-Practice Implementation Framework)** | Implementation science backbone for all early-care interventions — CFIR determinant assessment (what organizational/community factors enable/block childcare improvement?), RE-AIM outcome framework (reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, maintenance), fidelity measurement tools | **Meta-grant infrastructure:** All federal grants (Rare Impact, CMS RHT, NSF) require RPLICE assessment as condition of funding. RPLICE data becomes the evidence base for renewal/expansion grants. | RPLICE platform cost (~$5K–$15K setup, $2K–$5K/year maintenance per region) is offset by the grant-compliance value it provides (grants often require evaluation, which RPLICE automates) |
| **Ecosystem Nexus (Operational Intelligence)** | Cross-platform data integration for childcare ecosystem (provider data, family data, outcomes data, resource utilization), real-time operational monitoring (e.g., alert if a provider goes below capacity = shortage signal, trigger recruitment intervention) | Not directly grant-fundable, but provides operational data required by grants (reach numbers, implementation fidelity, equity metrics) | Reduces operational overhead (no separate evaluation contractor needed); provides real-time data for mid-course corrections |
| **Video Creator AI (Content Production)** | Grant-ready promotional videos, provider recruitment videos, family educational videos on childcare selection, subsidy eligibility, developmental milestones | Content production for Rare Impact Fund, CMS RHT, and foundation grants; required for many grants' public communication component | Professional content production cost savings (~$50K for 5 videos via outside vendor → $5K via TCAF AI tool) |

**TCAF funding model:** These platforms are open-source, research-grade tools available to any organization at **minimal implementation cost ($10K–$50K setup per region; $5K–$15K/year maintenance)** or as managed SaaS subscriptions. United Way can deploy the full TCAF stack for childcare ecosystem management at a fraction of commercial health-IT costs, while maintaining data sovereignty (data stays in Williamson County; TCAF does not monetize). This unlocks significant cost savings that can be reinvested in direct services (staff, subsidies, training).
