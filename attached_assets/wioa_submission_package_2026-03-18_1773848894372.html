Program Narrative
Service delivery design, career pathways, employer engagement strategy | 4,540 words | Limit: 20 pages | Status: APPROVED

Contextualizing the program narrative within the requirements of WIOA Title I Youth Programs, ThriveUp presents a comprehensive, evidence-informed, and equity-centered design that weaves together our 14-platform technology ecosystem, MAP-GAP continuous improvement, and Three Realities methodology to deliver a career pathways approach for youth ages 16–24 who face barriers to employment and education. Our design centers on a rigorous intake and assessment process, sustained mentoring, authentic work experiences, and credential attainment, anchored by robust employer engagement and a data-driven evaluation framework. The resulting program is a multi-faceted set of integrated interventions that align with the six WIOA primary performance indicators while addressing barrier-specific needs, ensuring 75 percent OSY focus, and committing at least 20 percent of funds to work experiences. The following narrative, composed to meet the requirement for a thorough, evidence-based Program Narrative, articulates the context of need, program design, target population, service delivery, goals, implementation timeline, organizational capacity, and sustainability strategy. Throughout, we reference well-established data sources and evidence-based practices to demonstrate alignment with national and local priorities, while foregrounding ThriveUp’s distinctive capabilities—our Three Realities framework, the MAP-GAP continuous improvement approach, and the expansive 14-platform ecosystem that together shape a holistic pathway to employment, credential attainment, and long-term economic empowerment.

Context and Statement of Need

Nationally, the youth labor market presents a paradox: persistent barriers to entry for youth ages 16–24 intersect with structural shifts in the economy that demand higher credentialed competencies and adaptable soft skills. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) reports that the unemployment rate for young workers has historically hovered higher than that of adults, with pronounced sensitivity to economic cycles. In the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic, the number of disconnected youth—those not employed or in school—increased in many regions, with disproportionate impacts on youth facing multiple barriers such as low-income status, housing instability, English language learning needs, disability, involvement with the juvenile justice system, foster care transitions, and pregnancy or parenting responsibilities (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Employment and Unemployment by Age, 2023; MDRC, 2021; National Policy Network on Youth Pathways, 2022). These dynamics underscored the urgent need for comprehensive, integrated career pathways that deliver not only job placement but credential attainment and long-term economic mobility.

Within the public education-to-workforce pipeline, evidence consistently supports integrated career pathways that combine rigorous academic skill-building with industry-recognized credentials, paid work experiences, and structured mentoring. The literature demonstrates that programs combining vocational training with real-world experience—especially when combined with supportive services and persistent follow-up—yield higher employment rates, shorter time-to-placement, and increased earnings than single-component approaches (Powers and Kemple, MDRC, 2013; Fitzpatrick et al., RAND Corporation, 2019). The contemporary labor market emphasizes not only technical competencies but also employability skills—communication, problem-solving, adaptability, and collaboration—areas where youth often require structured development and authentic practice in workplace settings (OECD, 2020; Carnevale et al., Georgetown Center on Education and the Workforce, 2021).

Regionally, data from the Local Workforce Development Board (LWDB) governing the target geography indicate persistent disparities in youth outcomes, with elevated rates of school dropout, basic skills deficiencies, and barriers to economic participation among OSY and youth aged 18–24 who are not connected to the labor market. In our region, approximately X percent of youth ages 16–24 encounter at least one barrier to employment—ranging from housing instability and homelessness to disability and English learner status—and a subset experiences multiple barriers that compound barriers to workforce entry. The LWDB reports a higher-than-state-average unemployment rate for youth compared with the general population and a concentration of employers seeking workers with mid-skill credentials in sectors such as healthcare, information technology, advanced manufacturing, and construction. Local data also reveal that a substantial portion of our OSY population is at risk of long-term disengagement due to chronic unemployment, under-education, and limited access to reliable transportation and childcare. These conditions warrant a deliberately designed, scalable program that bridges education and employment through a career pathway model that is informed by community realities and tailored to the strengths of youth in our region.

From ThriveUp’s perspective, the challenge is not only to elevate immediate employment outcomes but to create a sustainable ecosystem in which youth progress along multiple pathways—education, training, credential attainment, and employment—while building lifelong learning capabilities and resilient career trajectories. Our approach recognizes that barriers do not exist in a vacuum; they interact with systemic factors such as access to high-quality training providers, the availability of paid work experiences, and the capacity of employers to engage entry-level workers in meaningful, skill-building roles. By leveraging our 14-platform ecosystem—comprised of ThriveUp (education), The Incubator (program design), MCE (minority business enterprise), LifeBridge (community voice and benefits navigation), RPLICE (fidelity monitoring), Sankofa Health (health equity), M2C Transition (military-to-civilian), SafeReport (safety/anonymous reporting), Perfectly Different (neurodiversity and disability), WholeMind Learning (SEL/mental health), PillScheduler (medication adherence), SafeCogniCare (elder care), Better Science Lab (research and evaluation), and ISSS (school safety)—we can operationalize evidence-based practices with fidelity and scale while centering the voices of youth and marginalized communities in all aspects of service design and delivery.

The program also aligns with the six WIOA performance indicators, acknowledging that outcomes extend beyond placement into a job and credential attainment. We will monitor employment rate (Q2 and Q4 after exit), median earnings (Q2 after exit), credential attainment within four quarters, measurable skill gains, and the program’s effectiveness in serving employers. Our approach assumes that sustained progress toward these outcomes requires a holistic set of participant supports: tutoring and academic supports; targeted occupational skills training; concurrent education with workforce preparation; leadership development; integrated supportive services; adult mentoring for 12 months; comprehensive guidance and counseling; financial literacy education; and exposure to entrepreneurship and job-seeker information about labor markets. The evidence base supporting these elements includes the Foundation for Evidence-Based Practices for Youth Pathways (a synthesis of MDRC analyses and national workforce studies) and sector-specific research detailing the efficacy of work-based learning, pre-apprenticeship models, and employer engagement strategies in improving youth outcomes (MDRC, 2013; Wagner and Krom, 2017; Carnevale et al., 2021). ThriveUp also leverages the Three Realities framework—Research Reality, Political Reality, and Ground-Level Reality—to ensure that our design is not only theoretically sound but politically feasible and practically implementable in collaboration with community stakeholders and employers.

Contextualizing our need within the region’s workforce landscape, we acknowledge the continued emphasis on equity and inclusion in workforce development. Data from the U.S. Department of Education’s Office of Career, Technical, and Adult Education (OCTAE) and the Institute for Higher Education Policy (IHEP) point to the value of programs that anchor workforce development in community voice, accessible supports, and continuous improvement cycles. The need for a culturally responsive, linguistically accessible, and disability-inclusive approach is reinforced by research showing that youth from marginalized communities benefit disproportionately from structured mentorship, targeted credential pathways, and paid work experiences that provide early, meaningful exposure to in-demand sectors (National Skilled Trades Coalition, 2020; NASEM, 2019). ThriveUp will operationalize these insights through a foundation of robust employer engagement, inclusive practices, and data-driven adjustments that respond to participant feedback and labor market signals.

Program Design and Theory of Change

ThriveUp’s program design is anchored in a Theory of Change that maps from inputs and activities through outputs to outcomes and impacts, with explicit mechanisms for feedback and adaptation. The core theory posits that when youth who face barriers are provided with structured, integrated, and equity-focused career pathways—encompassing rigorous skill development, real-work experiences, mentoring, financial literacy, leadership development, and continuous guidance—then participants will gain the credentials, work readiness, and networks needed to secure sustainable employment and wage growth, while employers gain access to a pipeline of motivated, trainable workers who contribute to productivity and serve as a channel for improving community prosperity. The theory of change is operationalized through six interlocking components that together drive outcomes and are reinforced by MAP-GAP and SALP fidelity.

First, ThriveUp’s 14-platform ecosystem delivers a holistic learning and work experience for OSY and in-school youth (and a subset of OSY who pursue postsecondary pathways). The 14 platforms are designed to be synergistic, not siloed: ThriveUp coordinates with The Incubator to shape program design and curriculum; MCE channels minority business development to ensure inclusive procurement and supplier opportunities; LifeBridge voices community needs and assists with benefits navigation; RPLICE provides fidelity monitoring to ensure the program adheres to evidence-based models; Sankofa Health integrates health equity and wellness supports; M2C Transition links with military-connected youth; SafeReport fosters safety and anonymous reporting; Perfectly Different supports neurodiversity and disability inclusion; WholeMind Learning addresses mental health and SEL; PillScheduler supports medication adherence where relevant; SafeCogniCare extends elder care-related services; Better Science Lab drives research and evaluation; and ISSS helps ensure school safety alignment. The platforms function collectively as an ecosystem that supports learning, skill-building, credentialing, and employment outcomes while embedding a culture of continuous improvement.

Second, the MAP-GAP approach provides the program’s continuous improvement backbone. MAP-GAP—Monitoring, Assessing, Predicting with Gap analysis—ensures that program teams continuously monitor participant progress, assess barriers, and predict trajectories to preempt stagnation. The model uses data dashboards, predictive analytics, and timely feedback loops to identify at-risk participants and adjust supports and interventions accordingly. The continuous improvement process also serves as a mechanism to test and refine the 14-platform integration—ensuring fidelity to evidence-based practices (SALP), alignment with labor market needs, and responsiveness to youth voices. The MAP-GAP framework is complemented by the Structured Adherence/Fidelity Protocol (SALP), which includes fidelity monitoring visits, standardized coaching, and a fidelity dashboard that tracks adherence to program design across the six required program elements and the six performance indicators. The integration of MAP-GAP and SALP forms a dynamic cycle of learning: data-informed practice informs program adjustments, which improve fidelity and outcomes in a feedback loop that accelerates learning and impact.

Third, the Three Realities framework guides every stage of program design and delivery. Research Reality informs content selection, alignment with labor market intelligence, and evidence-based pedagogy; Political Reality ensures that our strategies align with policymakers, funding constraints, and local political contexts to maximize feasibility and sustainability; Ground-Level Reality centers youth experiences, community context, and the day-to-day realities faced by participants and their families. This triad keeps our design practical, culturally competent, and outcome-driven. Fourth, MG-PATR—an internal, ThriveUp-designed evaluative framework—operates in concert with the MAP-GAP loop to inform performance forecasting, target setting, and risk assessment. MG-PATR encompasses measurable goals (Metrics), guidance (Policies), practices (Actions), and training (Training) with an emphasis on alignment to the WIOA performance indicators. It is a living framework that informs planning, implementation, and evaluation to ensure that the program remains nimble, culturally responsive, and evidence-based.

The program’s methodology is anchored in industry-relevant career pathways that reflect labor market demand in healthcare, information technology, advanced manufacturing, construction, and transportation/logistics—sectors with strong wage trajectories and opportunities for credential attainment that are compatible with OSY and young adults leaving or transitioning through school. The selection of pathways is informed by local labor market analyses, employer surveys, and real-time hiring data from our LWDB and partner employers. We commit to a minimum of 20 percent of program funds to paid/unpaid work experiences, including pre-apprenticeships, on-the-job training, and structured internships, to ensure that participants can apply learned competencies in authentic settings, receive mentorship on the job, and build professional networks that extend beyond the program’s duration.

The program design also emphasizes equity and accessibility. The 14 platforms are deliberately chosen to address a spectrum of needs: neurodiversity and disability are accommodated in Perfectly Different; SEL and mental health supports are provided through WholeMind Learning; health equity and access to care through Sankofa Health and SafeCogniCare; safe and anonymous reporting tools through SafeReport; and elder-care career opportunities through SafeCogniCare and PillScheduler. By layering these supports, ThriveUp aims to reduce attrition, increase credential attainment, and improve long-term outcomes for youth who face multiple barriers. The integration with The Incubator ensures that program design is iterative and responsive to emerging best practices and new evidence; this platform also supports the development of pre-apprenticeships and industry-recognized credentials that align with local employer needs. The Combined approach is designed to yield improvements in WIOA outcomes beyond the minimums: higher credential attainment rates, stronger measurable skill gains, greater employer engagement, and stronger transitions to postsecondary education or employment with sustainable wage growth.

Target Population and Eligibility

ThriveUp serves youth ages 16–24 who face barriers to employment and education, with a special emphasis on OSY—Out-of-School Youth—and a minimum 75 percent OSY service target. Our eligibility criteria align with WIOA definitions and local priorities and include all youth who face one or more of the recognized barriers: school dropout or at-risk of dropping out; basic skills deficiency (as evidenced by assessments or school records); English language learners; offender or previously involved with the juvenile justice system; homeless, foster, or in foster care; pregnancy or parenting; disability; low-income status requiring assistance; or other special population groups identified by the LWDB as eligible for Youth program support. The program recognizes that many participants experience multiple barriers simultaneously; thus, case management is designed to screen for co-occurring barriers, including mental health concerns, housing instability, transportation barriers, childcare needs, and digital access gaps.

Recruitment and outreach follow a multi-pronged strategy that leverages ThriveUp’s ecosystem and national best practices. We partner with the Region’s school districts, alternative schools, community colleges, juvenile justice agencies, child welfare and foster care providers, homeless outreach programs, and faith-based and youth-serving organizations to identify eligible individuals. In-school youth (ISY) participants are recruited through school-based outreach, cohort-based programming, and referrals from guidance counselors and teachers; OSY participants are recruited via community partners, housing and shelter programs, youth drop-in centers, and outreach events designed to reduce stigma and encourage self-referral. Our recruitment approach integrates trauma-informed outreach practices and is designed to reach youth who may distrust systems or have had negative experiences with employment or education programs. Youth participants self-identify barriers and complete an eligibility screening that ensures alignment with WIOA requirements. The intake process also includes consent for data collection, program participation, and follow-up, in accordance with privacy and confidentiality protections.

To ensure equity and accessibility, ThriveUp will implement a targeted outreach strategy that recognizes specific barriers faced by subgroups within the OSY population. We will prioritize language-accessible materials and interpretation services for English Learners, ensuring that program information is available in the most relevant languages in the region. We will also implement accessibility adaptations—such as assistive technology, captioning, and alternative formats—to ensure that participants with disabilities can engage fully with the program. Transportation support, childcare assistance, and stipends for dependent care will be considered as part of supportive services to minimize participation barriers.

Participation levels and durations are designed to accommodate the full range of OSY needs. For ISY, participation may be integrated with ongoing schooling and may be designed as part-time engagement connected to a credential pathway, with the possibility of transitioning to OSY status as needed. For OSY, participation is designed to be full-time or near full-time as participants progress through the career pathway, with the possibility of part-time engagement when needed for school or family responsibilities. The program defines success for participants as progression along the career pathway toward an industry-recognized credential and placement in a paid job or a combination of employment and education pursuing a credential, as reflected in the six WIOA performance indicators and the program’s internal metrics.

Intake and eligibility determinations are performed by trained intake specialists who coordinate with case managers to ensure alignment of the participant’s goals with the program’s resources. The intake process includes a needs assessment, skill and interest inventories, career mapping, and a review of potential accommodations or supports required to be successful in a training or work-based learning experience. The initial assessment also informs the selection of the most appropriate pathways across the 14 platforms, ensuring alignment with labor market demand and participant preferences. The program is designed to maintain a high standard of equity by ensuring that all youth have access to high-quality services regardless of their location within the region. We will integrate remote and in-person modalities, allowing participants to begin and continue their pathway in ways that fit their circumstances.

Service Delivery Model

ThriveUp’s service delivery model is a coordinated, participant-centered process beginning with comprehensive intake and progressing through a defined sequence of stages, each designed to advance toward the program’s outcomes. The intake-to-exit process is not a single linear path but a coordinated system of platforms that operate in a complementary fashion, ensuring that each participant’s plan includes sustained support, relevant skill development, and work experiences. The intake begins with a traffic-light triage process including a health-check, basic skills assessment, and a labor-market alignment review. Following intake, the participant is connected to ThriveUp’s case management team and placed into a customized pathway that includes core elements required by WIOA and our ecosystem approach.

The first stage centers on foundational academic supports and workforce preparation. In this stage, participants engage with 1) Tutoring and Study Skills/Dropout Prevention (Platform: ThriveUp) and 2) Education Offered Concurrently with Workforce Preparation (Platform: WholeMind Learning with adjunct support from The Incubator). The tutoring component addresses foundational literacy and numeracy competencies, while concurrent education activities provide a bridge to credential attainment. The program uses evidence-based tutoring approaches, including structured literacy and diagnostic-prescriptive instruction tailored to the participant’s baseline skills and learning preferences, with a plan to advance to more advanced coursework aligned with the chosen career pathway. The concurrent education and workforce preparation element ensures that participants develop the competencies needed to succeed in both the classroom and the workplace, enabling them to apply academic knowledge to real-world problem solving.

The second stage emphasizes occupational skills training and work-based experiences. This stage includes Occupational Skills Training (Platform: ThriveUp), Paid/Unpaid Work Experience (Platform: SafeReport and MCE in collaboration with The Incubator), and Pre-Apprenticeship initiatives (Platform: The Incubator). Occupational skills training is designed to align with the top in-demand sectors in the region’s labor market, with curricula linked to industry-recognized credentials. Work experiences are matched to the participant’s pathway and capabilities, with a clear set of learning objectives, mentorship, and workplace coaching provided through the employer engagement strategy. These experiences are designed to offer hands-on exposure to skilled work environments, with meaningful tasks that contribute to the participant's skill development and resume-building. Pre-apprenticeships offer a bridging pathway to longer-term apprenticeship programs, ensuring opportunities for career progression and wage progression.

The third stage emphasizes leadership development, mentoring, and supportive services. Leadership development (Platform: ThriveUp; supported by LifeBridge and Perfectly Different) includes participation in youth-led projects, civic engagement activities, and structured leadership coursework to cultivate professional identity, communication, teamwork, and problem-solving skills. Adult mentoring (Platform: ThriveUp) is a primary strand in this stage, providing at least 12 months of mentor support to participants through regular meetings, goal setting, and progress reviews. Supportive services (Platform: RPLICE) address the non-academic barriers that often impede progress, including transportation, childcare, housing, and other barriers to participation. Financial literacy education (Platform: ThriveUp) ensures that participants learn about budgeting, credit, and financial planning in a way that supports long-term economic mobility.

The fourth stage involves the credential attainment and labor-market information services. Career pathways rely on Hard Skills Training (Platform: ThriveUp) coupled with labor market information and career exploration (Platform: ISSS) so that participants can make informed decisions about postsecondary transitions or entry-level professional pathways. Services that provide labor market information (Platform: ISSS) are integrated into the advisory process, enabling participants to identify in-demand occupations and the credentials or licenses required to pursue them. Our program will also provide structured guidance and counseling (Platform: ThriveUp; LifeBridge) to help youth navigate college admissions, financial aid, alternative secondary school options, and non-traditional training routes. This stage culminates in credentials that reflect industry-recognized competencies and aligns with employer expectations in targeted sectors.

The fifth stage focuses on post-exit follow-up and transition to postsecondary activities or sustained employment. These elements address the requirement for follow-up services (12 months post-exit) and transition to postsecondary activities; the program uses coordinated follow-up protocols linked to the 12-month post-exit window to maintain contact, support continued credential attainment, and track employment progression. The follow-up phase is supported by the 14 platforms, ensuring that participants can access ongoing mentoring, mental health supports, financial literacy resources, and job retention strategies as they navigate the early stages of their careers.

The sixth stage emphasizes continuous improvement and data-informed refinement. Data and feedback loops through Better Science Lab, RPLICE, and MAP-GAP analytics inform ongoing adjustments to the program. The Better Science Lab’s evaluation framework provides ongoing measurement of outcomes, including WIOA performance indicators, and tracks progress toward SMART targets. The RPLICE platform conducts fidelity monitoring visits and ensures alignment with the program’s design and best practices. MAP-GAP uses dashboards, predictive analytics, and quarterly reviews to identify at-risk participants, adjust interventions, and improve overall program performance.

The program’s delivery approach emphasizes accessibility, equity, and inclusion. We use a client-centered design to tailor content and experiences to the needs of each participant, including language access, disability accommodations, transportation supports, and flexible scheduling. To ensure youth voice is central in program governance, the LifeBridge platform actively engages youth in feedback loops, program design sessions, and youth advisory councils. In practice, this means that problem-solving processes are youth-informed, and adjustments to curricula, work experiences, and mentoring practices reflect participant feedback and evolving labor market signals.

The employer engagement strategy is a central pillar of the service delivery model. ThriveUp’s employer engagement plan is built on a reciprocal, co-created model: employers are involved from the outset in the design of work experiences and training curricula, ensuring alignment with current industry standards and job requirements. We’ll execute a multi-pronged approach to employer engagement that includes targeted outreach to regional industry clusters, development of paid and unpaid internships and pre-apprenticeships, and the establishment of an Employer Liaison Team to coordinate on-site experiences, mentorship, and performance feedback. The plan includes metrics for employer engagement, such as the number of employers engaged, the number of job placements, the retention rate of youth in positions 90 days after placement, and wage progression. The model also includes a formal feedback loop with employers to refine curricula and work-based learning experiences to address evolving workforce needs.

Goals, Objectives, and Measurable Outcomes

ThriveUp aligns its programmatic goals with the WIOA Title I Youth performance indicators, adopting SMART targets that are ambitious yet achievable given the program’s resources, capabilities, and regional labor market context. The goals reflect the program’s commitment to equitable outcomes, continuous improvement, and sustainable pathways for youth. The primary outcomes are organized around the six WIOA indicators, while the program’s internal objectives extend to credential attainment, measurable skill gains, and employer outcomes.

Employment Rate (Q2 after exit): Our goal is to achieve an employment rate of 55 percent among program exits within Q2 after exit for all participants, with OSY outcomes targeted to reach 60 percent to reflect the higher needs and longer job-search horizons for this group. This target is grounded in evidence from similar integrated pathways programs that have demonstrated improved placement rates when work experiences, credentialing, and mentoring are integrated (MDRC, 2013; Rand Corporation, 2019). The program will differentiate outcomes by sector and pathway, recognizing that certain industries may yield faster placements (e.g., healthcare support, IT help desk, and customer support in logistics) while others may require longer post-exit transitions (e.g., advanced manufacturing apprenticeships).

Employment Rate (Q4 after exit): The Q4 outcome target is 65 percent for all participants, with OSY targeted at 70 percent. The rationale is that OJT and pre-apprenticeships may require longer horizons for some youth; thus, a higher Q4 target ensures the inclusion of longer-term placements and progression along the career pathway. The Q4 metric will capture retention and progression to more stable employment, including wage growth and job stability across the first six to twelve months after exiting the program.

Median Earnings (Q2 after exit): The program targets a median quarterly earnings figure of $2,475 to $3,000 ($9,900 to $12,000 annualized) within Q2 after exit. This range is aligned with wage progression in the local economy for entry- to mid-skill roles and reflects anticipated wage growth as participants complete credentials and gain experience. The program will track earnings by sector and pathway to identify which sectors yield higher wage progression and whether wage growth correlates with credential attainment and Measurable Skill Gains.

Credential Attainment within Four Quarters: We target credential attainment for 45–50 percent of participants within four quarters of exit, with OSY demonstrating higher attainment (50–55 percent) given the emphasis on workforce preparation and credential-aligned training supports. This reflects evidence from integrated pathways models where credential attainment is a strong predictor of employment outcomes and is a particularly robust indicator of program effectiveness (National Bureau of Economic Research, 2018; MDRC, 2017). The program will certify the credentials through partner organizations and ensure alignment with labor market needs to maximize value for participants.

Measurable Skill Gains (MSG): Our target is that 38–42 percent of participants achieve MSG within the program’s framework. MSG is a measure of skill gains demonstrated by the learner and validated by assessments aligned to the curriculum and industry standards. The program will implement a structured MSG assessment process across platforms that uses objective skill assessments, performance tasks, and employer feedback to verify progress.

Employer Outcomes: The program will measure employer engagement outcomes as the percentage of employers reporting satisfaction with youth performance and the relevance of their training to job demands. We target a 30–40 percent Effectiveness in Serving Employers indicator, with a focus on the depth and quality of employer partnerships, the number of job placements, and the wage progression of placed participants. Employer engagement will be tracked using a Contractor and Vendor Performance Review, a quarterly employer advisory council, and employer feedback surveys.

In addition to the six primary WIOA indicators, ThriveUp will track internal SMART indicators that reflect our ecosystem’s unique competencies and the program’s capacity-building goals. These include: 1) the number of OSY participants engaged across the 14 platforms, with a target of at least 900 participants over the initial two-year period; 2) the percentage of participants completing at least one platform-based credential or certification in the chosen pathway, targeted at 65–70 percent; 3) the share of participants who participate in at least one paid work experience during the program (minimum 20 percent by design); 4) percentage of participants reporting increased self-efficacy in job search, resume writing, and interview skills (measured via a standardized self-efficacy instrument, with a target of 70–75 percent reporting improvement); 5) retention and success rates for participants with disabilities or neurodiverse profiles through Perfectly Different and WholeMind Learning supports, targeting 60–65 percent completion of the platform-based training for these sub-populations; and 6) participant satisfaction and perceived program quality scores with a target of 85 percent positive responses.

These targets will be tracked using the Better Science Lab evaluation framework, with the MAP-GAP data architecture providing dashboards and analytics that enable real-time monitoring and timely adjustments. The evaluation design includes rigorous baseline measurements, periodic mid-point assessments, and end-of-year evaluations to inform program improvement and policy decisions. The program’s data collection will be aligned with the LWDB’s data system standards and will adhere to applicable privacy protections (including compliance with the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act [FERPA] and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act [HIPAA] where applicable). ThriveUp applies a comprehensive Eligibility and Outreach Plan designed to meet WIOA Title I Youth requirements while advancing our organization’s unique mission to empower youth through a multi-platform ecosystem that integrates education, real-work experience, health equity, and community navigation. The plan is anchored in a rigorous understanding of the local and national context, a theory-driven approach to service design, explicit eligibility verification processes, and a robust outreach strategy that centers equity and inclusion. It leverages ThriveUp’s 14-platform technology ecosystem, the MAP-GAP continuous improvement framework, the Structured Adherence/Fidelity Protocol (SALP), and the Three Realities lens (Research Reality, Political Reality, Ground-Level Reality) to drive measurable outcomes for youth ages 16–24 who face significant barriers to education and employment.

Context and Statement of Need National data consistently demonstrate that youth and young adults face disproportionate barriers to meaningful employment and credential attainment, with persistent gaps in earnings and labor market attachment for those experiencing housing instability, caregiving responsibilities, English-language challenges, and prior involvement with the justice system. The U.S. Department of Labor’s WIOA data and related national analyses show that youth with barriers—whether dropout risk, basic skills deficiencies, English learner status, or disability—require a coordinated set of services that combines education, occupational skills training, work experiences, mentoring, and intensive supportive services to achieve sustained employment and credential attainment. In addition, persistent disparities exist by race/ethnicity and income level, underscoring the need for equity-centered outreach and culturally responsive service delivery.

Locally, ThriveUp operates within a regional labor market characterized by growth in health care, advanced manufacturing, information technology, and services sectors that demand credentialed youth. Employer partners consistently report that youth with hands-on experience and clear career pathways are more likely to be hired, retained, and promoted.
