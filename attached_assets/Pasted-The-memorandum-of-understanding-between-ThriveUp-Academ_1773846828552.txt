The memorandum of understanding between ThriveUp Academy and the Local Workforce Development Board establishes a formal framework for delivering WIOA Title I Youth programming that centers on career pathways, credential attainment, and meaningful employment for youth ages 16–24 who face barriers. This document integrates ThriveUp’s Three Realities methodology, MAP-GAP continuous improvement framework, SALP fidelity protocols, and our expansive 14-platform ecosystem to deliver a coherent, data-driven curriculum aligned with the six primary performance indicators and mandatory program elements defined by WIOA. Our approach centers on rigorous collaboration with the Workforce Development Board, local employers, school districts, community organizations, and cross-sector agencies to ensure that services are accessible, outcomes-driven, and sustainable beyond the grant period.

Context and Statement of Need. In our region, persistent youth unemployment and underemployment remain a barrier to long-term economic mobility. National data (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics) consistently show youth unemployment rates well above the overall unemployment rate, with pronounced disparities by race, ethnicity, geography, and educational attainment. Locally, longitudinal indicators indicate that youth aged 16–24 experience elevated rates of school disengagement, basic skills deficits, and barriers such as housing instability, foster care involvement, English learner status, disability, and delinquency histories. The local labor market features demand for skilled trades, healthcare support roles, information technology, and transportation/logistics; however, young people lacking credentials and work experiences struggle to access apprenticeship pipelines and paid placement. The need is particularly acute for Out-of-School Youth (OSY), who constitute the majority of our targeted population and who often lack coherent career pathways. Our proposed program responds to this need by delivering an integrated, mentored, work-based, and credential-focused experience that accelerates transition to postsecondary activities and gainful employment. The MOU reflects these realities by committing to serve at least 75% OSY, ensure no less than 20% of program funds support paid or unpaid work experiences, and align services with evidence-based approaches to youth workforce development, including structured leadership development, mentoring, financial literacy, occupational skills training, and labor market information services.

Program Design and Theory of Change. Our design follows a theory of change that begins with intensified outreach to OSY and In-School Youth (ISY) through school partners, juvenile justice systems, housing and shelter networks, and community-based organizations. Baseline assessments, gap analyses, and MAP-GAP-driven fidelity checks identify individual barriers and organizational bottlenecks, enabling tailored pathway design. The program uses a multilayered, platform-based ecosystem, with each platform contributing to one or more of the 14 required youth program elements: tutoring and dropout prevention, alternative secondary school services, work experience, occupational skills training, concurrent education with workforce preparation, leadership development, supportive services, adult mentoring, follow-up services for 12 months post-exit, comprehensive guidance and counseling, financial literacy education, entrepreneurial skills training, access to labor market information, and transition to postsecondary activities.

Our theory of change is anchored in evidence-based practices known to improve youth outcomes, including work-based learning, early credential attainment, mentor-supported development, and data-informed case management. We rely on published evaluations and guidance from the Department of Labor and national youth workforce studies showing that structured, paid or unpaid work experiences paired with sequential, competency-based training lead to higher Q2 and Q4 employment rates, stronger credential attainment, and greater earnings growth. ThriveUp’s MAP-GAP framework operationalizes continuous improvement by establishing baselines, monitoring performance against benchmarks, identifying gaps through gap analysis, and predicting barriers before they derail progress. SALP fidelity checks ensure that program delivery aligns with evidentiary standards for youth services, maintenance of participant trust, and fidelity to evidence-based intervention components. The Three Realities framework—Research Reality, Political Reality, and Ground-Level Reality—guides the program to adapt strategies to changing stakeholder contexts while preserving core elements proven to drive outcomes. Our 14-platform ecosystem—ranging from WholeMind Learning for SEL and mental health to PillScheduler for medication adherence and SafeReport for safety and anonymous reporting—enables a holistic approach to youth development, reduces attrition, and supports durable skill acquisition.

Target Population and Eligibility. The MOU targets youth 16–24 with barriers to employment and education, including but not limited to school dropout risk, basic skills deficiency, English language learner status, prior involvement with the juvenile or adult justice system, homelessness or housing instability, runaway or foster care experiences, parenting responsibilities, disability, and low-income status requiring assistance. Eligibility is defined by enrollment status (ISY or OSY), evidence of barriers, and willingness to participate in a structured program with regular assessments, mentoring, and work experiences. Recruitment strategies include formal partnerships with local high schools, community colleges, juvenile justice agencies, shelters and youth-serving organizations, employer partners for work-based learning, and targeted outreach through social media, community events, and referrals from case managers. The MOU commits to ensuring accessible enrollment without discrimination, providing accommodations under the Americans with Disabilities Act, and delivering culturally responsive services in multiple languages as needed by the local population.

Service Delivery Model. The service delivery model follows a step-by-step process beginning with intake and eligibility confirmation, followed by comprehensive assessments that map to the 14 program elements. During intake, youth complete a standardized barrier assessment, skill inventories, and an individualized service strategy that aligns with either an ISY or OSY pathway. The OSY pathway emphasizes accelerated credential attainment, paid/unpaid work experiences, and robust support services to re-engage disengaged youth, while the ISY pathway foregrounds concurrent education and workforce preparation to prevent dropout. The 14-platform ecosystem is activated through a coordinated sequence: WholeMind Learning supports SEL and mental health needs; PillScheduler ensures medication adherence where applicable; SafeCogniCare and Sankofa Health address health equity; LifeBridge amplifies community voice and benefits navigation; The Incubator guides program design and innovation; MCE drives minority business participation and supplier diversity; RPLICE provides fidelity monitoring; Perfectly Different ensures accessibility for neurodiverse learners; M2C Transition tailors military-to-civilian pathways; SafeReport provides safety and anonymous reporting; TPS (Transition to Postsecondary) aligns college/career goals; and the other platforms support financial literacy, leadership development, and entrepreneurship. Each platform’s role is tied to explicit milestones: education and training milestones, work experience placements, credential attainment, and measurable skill gains. The intake-to-exit cycle includes continuous progress reviews every 6–8 weeks, with quarterly career planning updates and annual follow-up contacts during the 12-month post-exit period. Data sharing with the Local Workforce Development Board is governed by a formal data-sharing agreement, with secure access to case management systems, performance metrics, and employer feedback, while safeguarding participant privacy.

Goals, Objectives, and Measurable Outcomes. The program sets SMART goals aligned with WIOA performance indicators and the MOU’s commitments. Target outputs over the program year include serving at least 430 youth (with a minimum of 320 OSY and 110 ISY, ensuring at least 75% OSY as required). At least 20% of total funds will support paid/unpaid work experiences, translating to approximately 80–100 individual work-based learning experiences if the annual budget is approximately $400,000. We anticipate an employment rate in Q2 after exit of 60% for all participants who exit into employment, rising to 65% by Q4; an employment rate for OSY specifically of 62% in Q2 and 68% in Q4 due to the intensified WEX exposure and employer engagement. Credential attainment within four quarters post-exit will target at least 40% of participants, with higher rates among those completing occupational skills training aligned to in-demand sectors. Measurable Skill Gains (MSG) will be demonstrated by a minimum of 45% of participants achieving at least one MSG prior to exit, with 60% for those enrolled in multi-semester training sequences. Median earnings in Q2 after exit will target an increase of at least 15% above the baseline wage of participants at enrollment, with OSY experiencing greater growth given integration with work-based experiences. Effectiveness in Serving Employers will be measured through employer satisfaction surveys achieving at least a 4.0 on a 5.0 scale, and a 35% placement rate into unsubsidized employment or registered apprenticeships within 12 months of exit. These outcomes will be tracked through the ThriveUp data system integrated with the LWDB’s performance dashboard, enabling real-time performance monitoring and rapid course corrections through MAP-GAP cycles. The logic model links program activities to outputs (number of work experiences, number of coaching sessions, number of credentials earned) and to outcomes (employment, earnings, persistence in postsecondary education, and transitions to apprenticeships or further training).

Implementation Timeline. The rollout proceeds in phased quarters to ensure solid governance, partner alignment, and readiness to scale. In Quarter 1, the MOU is finalized, data-sharing agreements are executed, and partner-led governance structures are established; platform onboarding begins with WholeMind Learning, LifeBridge, and RPLICE for fidelity monitoring. Quarter 2 emphasizes targeted outreach to OSY and ISY, formalized employer engagement through an Employer Advisory Board, and the launch of pilot cohorts in two in-demand sectors (healthcare support and information technology). Quarter 3 scales to three additional occupational tracks, expands work-based learning with paid internships or pre-apprenticeships, completes baseline MSG assessments, and initiates the MAP-GAP fidelity audits. Quarter 4 concentrates on credential attainment milestones, exit readiness, and 12-month post-exit follow-up planning, along with a comprehensive program evaluation and a plan for sustainability beyond the grant period. Key milestones include signing the MOU with the LWDB, completing initial MOUs with at least eight employer partners, achieving baseline performance against the six WIOA indicators, and establishing a quarterly review with the LWDB’s youth committee to ensure alignment with local labor market needs.
