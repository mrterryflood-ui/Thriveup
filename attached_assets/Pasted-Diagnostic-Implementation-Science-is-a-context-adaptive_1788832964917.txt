Diagnostic Implementation Science is a context-adaptive discipline for understanding reality, determining what should change, selecting the appropriate action for the conditions that actually exist, and continuously learning whether that action is producing the intended result. It is domain-agnostic but never context-agnostic. Healthcare, education, logistics, business, community development, defense, government, technology, grants, and contracting may require completely different methods and professional expertise. The discipline does not prescribe CFIR, RE-AIM, Agile, Lean, PMBOK, systems engineering, human-centered design, or any other framework as the answer. Instead, it provides the reasoning architecture for determining which questions need answering, which evidence is necessary, which tools fit the problem, what should happen next, and how the system should learn from what happens.

The 5Ws + H—Who, What, When, Where, Why, and How—are therefore not six questions. They are six interacting dimensions of inquiry. Each dimension must be examined through the others. We do not merely ask What are we doing? We ask: What are we doing, for whom, where, when, why, and how? Why this particular “what”? How was the “what” selected? Who defined it? When should it change? Where does it work or fail? Likewise, When? does not simply mean a schedule. We ask: When should this occur, for whom, under what conditions, where, why at that moment, and how will we recognize that the moment has arrived? Every answer can therefore become the subject of another diagnostic question.

This creates a 5W+H reasoning matrix rather than a 5W+H checklist. The Why behind the What examines the rationale for what we are doing. The How behind the When establishes how we know the appropriate moment has arrived. The Who behind the Why examines whose priorities, evidence, assumptions, or interests define the rationale. The Where behind the How asks whether a method that succeeds in one environment will function in another. The When behind the Who recognizes that stakeholder needs, authority, readiness, and capacity can change over time. And the What for Whom, Where, When, Why, and How forces us to define an intervention in relation to the people and conditions it is intended to serve. The dimensions intersect, and those intersections are often where the most important implementation knowledge is found.

That distinction prevents shallow answers to complex problems. Imagine a school district says, “We need an attendance intervention.” The diagnostic process cannot stop at what intervention, who receives it, when it starts, where it occurs, why attendance matters, and how it will operate. We must ask why attendance is declining, for whom, where, and since when; what explains differences among schools or populations; how we know those explanations are credible; whose perspective is missing; what happened before the decline; where existing interventions succeed; why they succeed there; whether the same mechanism applies elsewhere; how implementation itself might create burdens; and when evidence would justify adapting, scaling, stopping, or replacing the intervention. The problem becomes progressively clearer through interrogation rather than being prematurely reduced to a solution.

The same principle explains why two Walmart stores cannot be treated as identical implementation environments merely because they belong to the same corporation. A store in Wilmington, North Carolina, and one in Austin, Texas may share strategy, brand standards, technologies, and corporate objectives while differing substantially in customers, employees, regulations, climate, transportation, economics, competitors, supply chains, culture, and community conditions. The diagnostic question is not simply “How should Walmart implement the corporate strategy in each location?” It becomes: What must remain the same and why? What should differ and why? Different for whom? Where are the meaningful variations? When do those differences matter? How do we detect them? Who has authority to adapt? How much adaptation is appropriate? When does adaptation improve performance, and when does it undermine fidelity? The objective may be standardized while implementation remains intelligently localized.

This multidimensional reasoning also changes technology. A community intelligence system should not merely tell us what conditions exist. It should help determine what is happening to whom, where, since when, why it may be happening, how the conditions interact, who possesses relevant knowledge or resources, what has already been attempted, why previous efforts succeeded or failed, and when changing conditions warrant a different response. A grant system should not simply answer What funding opportunities exist? It should ask: Funding for what, for whom, from whom, why this opportunity, why this organization, why now, where will the work occur, how does it align with demonstrated need and organizational capacity, what will implementation require, how will performance be demonstrated, and when should the organization deliberately decide not to pursue it? A contracting system should interrogate mission need, customer, acquisition timing, capability, competition, teaming, geography, readiness, risk, past performance, regulatory conditions, and execution capacity through the same interconnected reasoning.

For organizations, this provides an alternative to management systems that mistake complexity for rigor. Diagnostic Implementation Science does not eliminate program management, project management, quality improvement, operations research, logistics, evaluation, or strategic planning. It organizes when and why those disciplines should be used. Instead of beginning with a methodology, the organization begins by establishing reality. What is happening? To whom? Where? Since when? Why do we believe it is happening? How do we know? What do we not know? Who knows something we do not? Why does the gap matter? What would success mean, and to whom? Where should intervention occur? When? Why there and then? How will it be implemented? What dependencies exist? How will it be maintained? Who owns it? How will we know whether it worked? Why did the result occur? What changed elsewhere because of it? What happens next?

The architecture must also distinguish facts, evidence, assumptions, hypotheses, unknowns, and disagreements. A diagnostic system should not manufacture certainty merely because a question has been asked. Sometimes the correct answer to Why? is we do not yet know. That answer should trigger the next inquiry: What would we need to know? Why is it unknown? Who might know? Where could evidence be obtained? How reliable would that evidence be? When do we need it? What can safely occur before we have it, and what decisions should wait? Uncertainty therefore becomes something the system manages rather than conceals.

The resulting implementation process is dynamic rather than linear. Sense → Question → Understand → Diagnose → Decide → Act → Measure → Learn → Adapt provides an operating rhythm, while the interconnected 5W+H architecture continuously interrogates each stage. At Measure, for example, we ask: Measure what? Why? For whom? By whom? Where? When? How? Why that measure rather than another? How frequently? What threshold matters? Who decides that threshold? What happens when it is crossed? At Adapt, we ask what should change, why, for whom, where, when, how much, who authorizes it, how its effects will be measured, and what must remain unchanged. No stage is protected from questioning.

This is also where human judgment becomes essential. The system should help determine not merely when humans enter a decision but why human judgment is required, what decision requires it, who possesses the appropriate expertise or authority, where accountability resides, how much discretion is appropriate, what information that person needs, how the decision is documented, and when that decision should be reconsidered. Automation and artificial intelligence should therefore augment diagnostic capacity rather than replace accountability.

The central principle is adaptive fidelity: preserve what must remain true while adapting what must respond to reality. Implementation is always local even when strategy is global. Standardization protects essential purpose, requirements, safety, ethics, and outcomes. Adaptation recognizes legitimate differences among people, places, organizations, resources, cultures, environments, and moments in time. Diagnostic Implementation Science determines the boundary between them through evidence and continuous inquiry rather than assumption.

Ultimately, Diagnostic Implementation Science is a discipline for knowing what to think about, how to think about it, what to do with what we learn, and when our understanding itself needs to change. Its power does not come from asking more questions for the sake of complexity. It comes from asking the next necessary question. Who, What, When, Where, Why, and How form an interconnected diagnostic architecture in which any dimension can interrogate any other dimension. That architecture allows an individual, organization, community, or intelligent system to move from information to understanding, understanding to decision, decision to action, action to evidence, and evidence back to better understanding. That is how implementation becomes not simply a project methodology, but a continuous capacity for learning and achieving results in the real world.
